# Star Realms: design

Decisions for adding Star Realms to this repo, agreed in a design (grill) session on 2026-10-17. Change them deliberately: update this file in the same PR as the code that departs from it.

## Goal

A **strong Star Realms bot**, and an understanding of *why* it is strong. The project exists to learn about search, evaluation and learned play by exploring them on a game where it is not obvious which matters most. No technique is the point, minimax included.

"Strong" is relative, not a fixed bar: the aim is always to **raise the win rate against every other approach** built so far. There are two kinds of step, and the project balances them like an exploration-exploitation trade-off:

- **Refine** an approach that is working: its evaluation, budget, parameters.
- **Try a different approach** when refinement flattens out or the evidence says the current one is limited by its form, not its settings.

Approaches are reasoned from first principles and built here. Other projects are evidence for what might work, not designs to reproduce. Hand-crafting (an evaluation, a policy, a planner) is as legitimate as search or learning when it is the right tool, and it is often the baseline that a general method has to beat.

Win rates are measured on **seeded bot-vs-bot games**. Each candidate plays a fixed set of seeded games (e.g. 500, alternating seats, paired seeds) against a ladder of baselines and the best of each other approach. It is stronger if it wins clearly more than 50%, reported with the budgets used, the measured time per move, and any training compute spent before play.

The ladder of baselines:

- **random**: a random legal action.
- **greedy**: play everything, buy the most expensive affordable card (repeatedly), attack the opponent directly, destroying the weakest Outpost first while Outposts guard them; never scrap (from its cards or the Trade Row) or use a destroy-base ability; take the "or" option with the most trade plus combat (so never draw or recycle); put each ship it buys on top of its deck when it may, and take the most expensive free ship (Blob Carrier); copy the ship with the most trade plus combat (Stealth Needle) and scrap nothing with Brain World; discard the cheapest card when made to. `GreedyBot`'s docstring has the exact priority order.
- **heuristic**: a hand-written buying and attacking policy: faction synergy, economy early and combat late, valuing bases, scrapping Explorers (and anything with a combat scrap ability when that wins), scrapping Vipers and Scouts whenever a card lets it scrap from hand or discard pile (from the discard pile first, the one it least wants to play first), destroying bases before hitting the player and Outposts sensibly, using each free destroy-base ability on the opponent's base with the most defense, scrapping from the Trade Row (after buying) the card the opponent would most want unless the average card left in the Trade Deck would likely suit them better, "or" choices (trade early and combat late, Authority when low, Blob World's draw once it draws at least 3 cards, Recycling Station's recycling of Vipers and Scouts while it holds any), putting each ship it buys on top of its deck when it may and taking the free ship it would most want to buy (Blob Carrier), scrapping up to two Vipers and Scouts with Brain World (from the discard pile first), copying the ship it would least want to give up (Stealth Needle), and, when made to discard or to scrap from hand, giving up the card it least wants to play (a Viper early, a Scout late). `HeuristicBot`'s docstring has the exact priority order.

## End state

The player the work is moving towards. Without a target, each step can only be judged against the last champion, and the work drifts into a chain of narrow head-to-head wins; with one, each step tests a belief the target rests on, and its result says whether the target still stands. Building one part at a time stays the method, because it is how a gain is attributed to its cause; the end state says which parts are on the path.

It is **technology-agnostic**: it says what the player knows and does, and why, never how it is computed or in what. The sections below (Search, Compiled engine, Learned values) describe how today's player is built; a technology appears there, chosen to serve a part of the end state, and can change without the end state changing.

Each part states what the player does, the **belief** it rests on (a claim about Star Realms or about choosing moves, true or false whatever the implementation), and the cheapest test of that belief. A belief that fails its test changes the end state, here, in the same PR as the result.

One constraint binds every part: **the player must learn at the speed the feedback loop needs.** Learning is the source of strength (How it chooses), and an idea is worth only as much as the experiments it can be tested in, so a description that is rich but slow to compute or to learn from is a bad trade against a plainer one that allows more games and more experiments. Each part is built in the form that keeps it fastest to train, and a part that can't be is a cost to justify, not a default.

### How it chooses

The player's strength is in **learned judgement**, and lookahead refines it. It acts on its judgement through the smallest lookahead that turns judgement into moves, comparing the positions its own turn can end in, and looks further only where a measurement says it pays.

- **In this kind of game, most of the strength comes from learned judgement; lookahead on top adds a little, and soon stops adding.** In the record ([learning-and-search.md](learning-and-search.md), 3.2), search on a learned judge gains tens of Elo and saturates within a small budget; here every gain since the whole-turn planner came from its value. *Test:* on the champion, the gain from four times the examination budget on the turns where it runs out, and from adding the opponent's reply at equal cost (Learned values: looking through the reply). Both are expected to be small; a large one moves strength towards lookahead. *Measured* for the examination (#187; results, Milestone 9: more examination where the budget runs out): `pf01` at 80,000 states per plan against itself at 20,000 wins 50.8% of 600 paired games (95% interval 46.8-54.8%, sign z 1.04) at 2.9× the time per plan, so more examination adds no measurable strength, as expected. *Measured, the reply* (#188; results, Milestone 9: looking through the reply): at equal cost (80,000 states per plan), `pf01` looking through the reply won 44.7% (39.1-50.3%) against itself at 16 worlds, seeds won both / split / lost both 24 / 86 / 40 (sign z −2.00), about where the plain 4-world planner stands against 16; and the reply changed its choice on 33.7% of plans, three times a fresh 4-world sample's 11.3%. So looking through the reply adds no strength (if anything it costs some) while overruling the judgement often: the judgement summarises the reply better than this lookahead does.
- **Within the turn, lookahead is cheap and exact, so tactics are calculated, not learned.** Everything that decides a turn's tactics (lethal, the order of plays and scraps, where combat goes) is visible to the mover except the cards a draw or reveal will bring, and almost every turn's outcomes can be examined in full. *Test:* the share of turns examined in full, and how often a turn's choice changes when the rest are. *Measured* (#185; results, Milestone 9: the judgement's errors by reshuffle distance and rare cards, and turns not examined): at 20,000 states per plan, 18% of `pf01`'s turns aren't examined in full, none in turns 1-6 but 22% in 13-18, 40% in 19-24 and 57% from turn 25. So "almost every turn" holds early and not late. *Measured* (#187; results, Milestone 9: more examination where the budget runs out): 11% of `pf01`'s plans run out of their 20,000 states (none in turns 1-5, 18% in 16-20, 31% in 26-30); planned again from the same worlds with four times the states, 24% of those choose a different line, so about 2.6% of all plans, and four times the states examines only 44% of them in full. So the turns that aren't examined in full are common late, but examining them further rarely changes the choice.
- **Beyond the turn, hidden cards are chance, not strategy.** No player gains by hiding intent, so judging a few sampled worlds is enough, and looking through the opponent's reply mostly averages over their luck: the cheat, which sees every card and looks two turns ahead, only ties the heuristic. *Test:* how much the choice changes with more sampled worlds. *Measured* (#186; results, Milestone 9: more sampled worlds): at 16 worlds instead of 4, `pf01`'s line changes on 8.7% of plans, fewer than a fresh sample of 4 changes it (11.3%), so what changes is mostly near-ties; 16 worlds against 4 won 54.3% (48.7-59.9%) of 300 paired games, sign z 1.90, at 4.0× the time per plan. *Settled* (#210; results, Milestone 10: 16 worlds against 4, settled): an SPRT of 16 worlds against 4 (H0 50%, H1 55%, α = β = 0.05) accepted H0 after 200 games, 16 worlds winning 43.5% (36.8-50.4%) at 4.2× the time per plan; with #186's games, 250 of 500, exactly 50%. The belief holds: the choice changes little and strength not measurably, so 4 worlds stays, and none of the uses of extra budget measured pays.

### What it judges

The player's learned judgement is of **positions**: how likely the player who has just ended their turn is to go on to win from the position their turn ended in. Its policy over whole turns is defined by that judgement (play the turn whose end it judges best), not learned beside it. A learned **preference over moves** would exist only to decide which outcomes are examined where they can't all be, drawing on the same understanding of the game as the judgement of positions and learning from how the player judged every outcome it compared, not only the one it chose. **It is not needed on today's evidence**: examining more on the turns too large to examine changes few choices and wins nothing (the first belief below), so the two beliefs below about a move preference wait until a measurement gives a move preference a place.

- **Where a move's immediate result is known, judging the position it leads to is the efficient way to judge the move** (afterstates; [research/policy-representation.md](research/policy-representation.md)). Many moves lead to the same position, so each judgement teaches about all of them, and a perfect simulator gives the position of every move. The established reasons for a separate move policy don't hold here: there is a simulator; hidden information is chance, so no deliberately mixed strategy is needed; and almost every turn's outcomes can be examined in full. The one that may hold is the turns too large to examine, which is the move preference's only job. *Test:* How it chooses, the first test: if examining more on those turns changes little, the end state has no preference over moves. *Measured* (#187; results, Milestone 9: more examination where the budget runs out): four times the examination changes 2.6% of `pf01`'s choices and adds no measurable strength (50.8%, 46.8-54.8%), so the preference over moves is **not needed**: ordering the examination better can't be worth more than examining four times as much, which reaches every line the smaller budget did. Re-measured on each new champion.
- **The decisions that win a deckbuilder are investments.** Buying, scrapping and when to attack trade the present for a better position later, so their worth is the worth of the position they lead to.
- **A judgement of positions can be checked and explained.** A position goes on to win or it doesn't, so the judgement is tested against what happens; and it can be taken apart by its causes (the deck, the race, timing), which the Goal's *why* needs. A preference over moves says only what to do.
- **Where both exist, they share one understanding of the game.** Learning to judge positions and learning to prefer moves draw on the same facts about the game. In Go, with search, one judgement learned for both played about 600 Elo stronger than two learned apart, which its authors put down mainly to each task regularising the other (research/policy-representation.md, 3). That is evidence from another game, with deep search, so here it is a belief to test, not a finding. *Test:* only once a move preference has earned its place.
- **A move preference learns from the player's whole comparison, not its verdict.** Where two outcomes were judged nearly equal, preferring the other is a small error, not a wrong answer. In expert iteration, learning from the search's spread of judgements played about 50 Elo stronger than learning its chosen move, at the same prediction error (research/policy-representation.md, 4).

- **The judgement knows how sure it is.** Beside each position's chance of winning, it gives how confident it is in that chance, so that the player can explore where it is unsure while learning and be wary there in matches (How it explores). *Belief:* a position can be judged uncertain for two different reasons, that it is genuinely a coin flip or that the judgement hasn't learned enough about positions like it; only the second is worth exploring or being wary of, so the confidence is about the judgement, not the game. *Test:* positions judged least confident should be where the judgement's errors are largest.

### What it sees

The judgement sees the **public position** only, which every sampled world shares, described in the game's low-level facts: what each card is and where it is, not what it means. Higher-order ideas about the game ([star-realms/strategy.md](star-realms/strategy.md)) are left for the judgement to learn, and asking whether it has is how its understanding is judged (below).

- **Each player's cards, by where they are in the deck's cycle**: the discard pile, the cards still to be drawn before the next reshuffle (deck and hand together, as a set), the ships in play and the bases in play. *Belief:* when a card will be drawn matters as much as owning it; a card bought just before a reshuffle is in the next hand, one bought after it waits a whole cycle. *Test:* the judgement's error on positions near a reshuffle against those far from one, with and without the split. *Measured without it* (#185; results, Milestone 9: the judgement's errors by reshuffle distance and rare cards, and turns not examined): `pf01`'s gap is near zero both near and far, and its squared miss differs by no consistent direction within game phases, so the error the split would remove isn't visible, and the split is **not needed** on today's evidence. Re-measured on each new champion; built only if the error near a reshuffle grows.
- **Cards as proportions as well as counts.** *Belief:* a card's worth is diluted by the rest of the deck, because what matters is how often it turns up in a hand. The hand-made features, which put proportions back, are the one change that has raised strength since `pi03` (Learned values, below).
- **Cards by what they do, as well as by name.** *Belief:* cards that do similar things are worth similar amounts, so what is learned about common cards should carry to rare ones. *Test:* the judgement's error on positions with a rare card against those without. *Measured* (#185; same results): within the game phases that have both, positions holding a rare card are judged a little better than those without, not worse, so this diagnostic doesn't call for describing cards by what they do; holding any of 23 rare cards is a broad test, which a few misjudged cards could hide in. The same-function synergy probe gives this part another reason: a judgement that knows cards only by name hasn't built the worth of a second card that draws or scraps (How its understanding is judged), and knowing what each card does is what would let it.
- **Both players described alike**, with whose turn is next. *Belief:* the game is symmetric apart from tempo, so what makes a deck good is the same for either player.
- **The Trade Row as it bears on whoever buys next.** *Belief:* a Trade Row card is worth what it would add to that player's deck, given their trade and factions; without that, denial can't even be represented. *Test:* the denial probe's share seen and the share of buys that deny, with and without it. *Measured without it* (#192; results, Milestone 9: probe, denial): `pf01` sees about 2% of a row card's worth to the next buyer in its being there for them, and its buys deny no more often than chance, so the judgement holds denial only weakly.
- **Never the order of anything hidden.** The judgement scores the position, not the sampled world (today's design, kept).

### What it learns from

The judgement learns from positions, each paired with **what came of it, many turns on**: the game's result, or a later judgement that itself carries the rest of the game, or the result of playing the position out. How the consequence is obtained is open; that it reaches far into the future is not. It learns from three kinds of position:

- **The positions it played.** *Belief:* this is the one route by which long-range consequences reach the player at all. Its lookahead ends with its own turn (How it chooses), so anything that pays off later (an investment in trade, a faction built up over several buys, a card denied) is weighed only through its judgement, and the judgement knows only what its learning carried. A strong human plans far ahead cheaply by intuition; this is the player's equivalent, and a consequence that never reaches the learning can't be learned. Whether the consequence is the bare result or a chain of later judgements is a refinement: on the evidence so far (Learned values, temporal-difference targets), it changes little at full data.
- **A sample of the positions it compared and rejected**, above all those it judged nearly as good as its choice, with what would have come of them. *Belief:* choosing the best of many noisy judgements favours the overestimated ones, so the errors that cost games are in the positions the player chooses between, which positions played alone never correct; held-out accuracy on played positions not predicting play is the symptom. *Test:* the **exploitation gap**, the champion's judgement of the positions it chose against how often it went on to win from them, measured on games already played. A gap near zero says this source isn't needed. *Measured* (#184; results, Milestone 9: the exploitation gap): `pf01`'s gap on 17,900 positions it chose is +0.002 ± 0.005, calibrated within 0.02 in every tenth of judged chance, with a small gap only in games past turn 24 (+0.033 ± 0.012). So for today's player this belief fails and the source is **not needed**: the positions it chose are judged as well as they turn out, so there is no selection error for rejected positions to correct. The gap is re-measured on each new champion, since another judgement could select differently.
- **Positions from games against opponents unlike itself** (the heuristic, earlier champions). *Belief:* a judgement learned from its own games alone has gaps that an opponent with another style finds.

### How it explores

While learning, the player deliberately plays some turns whose outcome it is **unsure** of: those it judged nearly as good as its choice, and those its judgement is least confident about. In matches it plays its best judgement, and is wary of outcomes it is unsure of. The project balances refining an approach against trying another (Goal); the player balances the same two things inside its own learning.

- **A self-taught player learns only about the strategies it tries, and strategies whose payoff comes late are the least tried.** Early in learning the judgement can't yet see an investment's payoff, so the player doesn't invest, and never plays the games that would show it the payoff: it can stay a racer because it never explored, not because racing is right. *Test:* the investment probe (How its understanding is judged) on two players learned alike, one exploring and one not; if the explorer leans further towards trade and plays stronger, exploration was the limit. *Measured on one player* (#191; results, Milestone 9: probe, investment against the race): `pf01` values trade early and combat above it from then on, and buys that way, which is how strong players play (star-realms/strategy.md, The race and the investment); so the probe shows no sign today that a lack of exploration has left it a racer, and the two-player test waits until exploration is built.
- **Exploration is directed, not random.** Almost every one of a turn's possible ends is plainly worse than the best few, so a random one teaches little; near-ties and positions the judgement is unsure of are where another choice might be right. *Test:* at equal learning cost, directed against random exploration, by strength and by the probes.
- **In matches, being unsure is a reason for caution.** Choosing the best of many judgements favours the overestimated ones (What it learns from), and an outcome the judgement is unsure of is the likeliest to be overestimated. *Test:* choosing by the judgement less its uncertainty against the judgement alone, by strength and by the exploitation gap.

### How its understanding is judged

Strength is judged by win rates (Goal); understanding by **probes**: questions put to the trained judgement, each comparing positions that differ in one fact, about the ideas in [star-realms/strategy.md](star-realms/strategy.md):

- **Same-function synergy**: does a second card that draws, scraps or makes the opponent discard add more to the judged chance of winning than the first did?
- **Faction pairs**: how much does a card of one faction add to a deck mostly of another, over every pair of factions; does the judgement single some pairs out?
- **Investment against the race**: through the game, how does the judgement weigh one more trade per hand against one more combat per hand, and does the player's buying shift towards trade as it improves, against the heuristic's?
- **Denial**: is a position judged worse when a card the opponent would value is in the Trade Row for them to buy, and does the player ever buy or scrap a card to deny it?

A probe takes real positions, every turn's end in the champion's games against itself, and changes one fact in each, keeping it a valid position (a card added to a deck is a copy out of sight, held by neither player and not in the Trade Row), so the judgement is asked about positions like those it plays, not invented ones that may lie outside what it learned (`probe`, `python -m minimax_games.games.star_realms probe`). Each reports the judged gain in chance and in logit: a judgement linear before its sigmoid gains less in chance as the chance nears 1, which would read as a missing idea where there is none. And each carries a control, a change that isn't the idea (for synergy, cards of no function), because any card is diluted by the cards beside it, so only the difference from the control speaks for the idea. On `pf01` (results, Milestone 9: the baseline, and each probe's section): same-function synergy isn't learned for cards that draw or scrap, and only a little, early, for discard; of faction pairs, a card adds most to a deck of its own faction and only one cross pair is singled out, slightly (Machine Cult cards in Trade Federation decks); its judgement sees about 2% of what a Trade Row card is worth to the next buyer in its being there for them, its Trade Row scraps mostly deny, more often than the heuristic's scripted ones, and its buys don't; and it values trade early and combat above it from then on, and buys that way. So two ideas are missing from its judgement, same-function synergy and the Trade Row as it bears on the next buyer, and the other two show a coherent view.

An idea the probes find the judgement hasn't learned becomes the next experiment: describe it to the judgement directly. If that adds strength, the player couldn't build the idea for itself, which is what the hand-made features showed for proportions; if it adds nothing, the idea may not matter at this level of play. Either way the result goes in the strategy doc, turning lore into a measurement. Describing ideas directly is legitimate in today's player (the Goal allows hand-crafting); the end state leaves them out only so that whether they are learned stays a question that can be answered.

### How progress is measured

Every experiment reports three things, all cheap enough to stay inside the feedback loop:

- **Strength**, against a fixed **reference population** (the heuristic and every champion), on fixed seeds paired with each opponent's recorded results on them: the win rate against each, and their mean. Every member plays at full speed, so a run takes minutes; the planner on `evaluate` is left out because it plays only at the speed of the reference engine, which would put tens of minutes into every run, and every champion is above it (`mlp` beat it), so its recorded results stand without it. A champion is promoted when it loses to none of them and beats the current one head to head. A population, not only the last champion, because a chain of head-to-head wins can each be narrow and still not add up to playing better against everything. It is played by one command (`population`, README; #183): 500 games against each member but the player itself, on seeds 0-499 for every member, the player first on even seeds, all in one Rust batch, so the results are the same on any number of threads. The seeds are the ladder's, so a player's match against the heuristic is its ladder match and its recorded ladder results check the command; one set for every member, because pairing needs only that a candidate and the champion met the same opponent on the same seeds, and the seeds aren't trained on (training, held-out and gating games use other ranges). The champion's per-seed results are committed in `docs/population/<champion>/<opponent>.csv` (the form gating and ladder matches use), and a candidate's run is paired with them seed by seed (`--paired`): the seeds won only by each, and the sign test's z, which cancel the luck of the deal that two independent samples would carry. 500 games put each rate within about ±4 points, and the champion's run takes minutes. `pf01`'s baseline: 74.0% against the heuristic, 61.6% against `mlp`, 56.6% against `pi03`, mean 64.1% (results, Milestone 9: the reference population and pf01's baseline).
- **Understanding**: the probes above, on the trained judgement. They judge constructed positions and play no games.
- **Diagnostics**, on games already played: the exploitation gap, the judgement's error near a reshuffle against far from one and on positions with a rare card, and the share of turns not examined in full.

An experiment names the end-state belief it tests and predicts its effect on strength and on one probe or diagnostic, and it is a result when both predictions are met. Strength that moves without its diagnostic is recorded as unexplained; a diagnostic that moves without strength says the belief is true but doesn't limit play. Results that only report a win rate are what let the work drift into a chain of narrow wins that explained nothing.

**Time goes to moving towards the end state**, not to refining a player that isn't it. Hand-crafted players still earn a place, as **opponents**: a player with an idea described to it directly (a hand-made feature, a scripted denial) joins the reference population, so a player nearer the end state has to learn to beat it, and may learn the idea itself by playing against it. That is a reason to build one, and the only one; making it the champion is not.

## Rules and scope

- Base set only: an 80-card Trade Deck across four factions (Blob, Trade Federation, Star Empire, Machine Cult), plus Explorers, Scouts and Vipers. No expansions.
- Built in **slices**. The first slice used only cards whose effects are trade, combat, authority, draw, and ally abilities limited to those. Later slices each added one kind of effect: opponent discards, destroy target base, scrap from hand/discard pile, scrap from the Trade Row, "or" choices, next ship acquired goes on top of the deck, and last the one-off abilities (Embassy Yacht, Brain World, Stealth Needle, Fleet HQ, Mech World). Every base-set card is now playable.
- Rules references: [RulesPal rulebook](https://rulespal.com/star-realms/rulebook), [Wise Wizard rulebook](https://files.wisewizardgames.com/wwg/rules/SRROE_Rulebook_Web_23-08-24.pdf).
- **The card list is a data file** (e.g. `cards.toml`) transcribed from online sources, with the sources recorded in the file. It holds game data only (name, cost, faction, type, defense, primary/ally/scrap effects, copies), not flavour text or art. It is cross-checked against `../py-realms` (names, costs, counts only; don't reuse its implementation). It lives at `src/minimax_games/games/star_realms/cards.toml` and is loaded by `catalogue.py`. Tests check the per-faction counts, the total of 80, and that every effect named in the file parses into the `Effect` enum (unknown effects fail loading).
- **The playable Trade Deck** is every card whose primary, ally and scrap effects the rules all implement. One set lists the implemented kinds of effect, `catalogue.IMPLEMENTED`, and each card slice adds its own to it: `catalogue.playable(card)` and `catalogue.playable_deck()` follow from it, as do the abilities `rules` offers. `new_game` deals `catalogue.PLAYABLE_TRADE_DECK` by default. With only the first slice's kinds (trade, combat, authority, draw) it is the 30-card first slice (a test pins this); with opponent discards, 40 cards (Imperial Fighter, Imperial Frigate, Survey Ship and Royal Redoubt join; Battlecruiser waits for destroy-base); with scrapping from hand or discard pile, 52 cards (Trade Bot, Missile Bot, Supply Bot, Battle Mech, Junkyard and Machine Base join; Patrol Mech waits for "or" choices); with destroy-base, 56 cards (Command Ship, Port of Call, Battlecruiser and Missile Mech join; Blob Destroyer waits for scrapping from the Trade Row); with scrapping from the Trade Row, 60 cards (2 Battle Pods and 2 Blob Destroyers join); with "or" choices (`Choice`, and `DrawPerFactionCard` and `DiscardThenDraw`, which only appear inside them), 70 cards (2 Trading Posts, 2 Barter Worlds, Defense Center, 2 Patrol Mechs, Blob World and 2 Recycling Stations join); with ships on top of the deck (`NextShipOnTop`, `AcquireShipFreeOnTop`), 74 cards (2 Freighters, Central Office and Blob Carrier join); with the one-off abilities (`DrawIfBases`, `ScrapThenDraw`, `CopyShip`, `ShipsGainCombat`, `AllyForAllFactions`), all **80 cards** (2 Embassy Yachts, Brain World, Stealth Needle, Fleet HQ and Mech World join), so `PLAYABLE_TRADE_DECK` is the whole Trade Deck (a test pins this) and `rules` no longer checks which effects are implemented. A `Choice` counts as implemented only when every effect of every option is (`catalogue.implemented_effect`).

## Where it lives

- `src/minimax_games/games/star_realms/`: engine, cards, bots, match runner and tournament.
- `src/minimax_games/search/` grows beyond minimax (ISMCTS joins `alphabeta`).
- Star Realms stays in this repo. The repo explores search in the broad sense, game-tree search, planning, constraint search, and learning a value or policy, across several games, and Star Realms is one of them. Parts that need speed move to Rust inside this repo (Compiled engine), with Python orchestrating; that is a per-component choice, not a reason for a separate project.

## Engine

- **Immutable state**: frozen dataclasses with tuples for piles. A `Card` hashes by its name (unique in the catalogue), which is cheap and agrees with equality. `apply(state, action) -> state` returns a new state sharing unchanged parts. States are hashable, so transpositions can be detected.
- **superenums** for the closed sets, with every `match` checked for completeness:
  - `Action`: `PlayCard`, `UseAlly`, `UseBase`, `Scrap`, `Acquire`, `AttackPlayer`, `AttackBase`, `DestroyTarget`, `Discard`, `ScrapHandCard`, `ScrapDiscardCard`, `ScrapTradeRowCard`, `Choose`, `Recycle`, `AcquireOnTop`, `AcquireFreeShip`, `ScrapAndDraw`, `Copy`, `EndTurn`.

    Bases stay in play across turns, in their own per-player pile, and count as in play for ally abilities and scrapping. A base's primary ability is used once in each of its owner's turns: playing the base uses it that turn, and `UseBase` uses it on later turns. It is an explicit action, not applied at the start of the turn, because when to use it can matter (draws, and the bases with choices or targets); automatic play of pure-gain abilities takes it over like any other action. `AttackBase` spends combat equal to a base's defense and puts the base in its owner's discard pile. `AttackPlayer` spends all the combat. While a player has an Outpost, only Outposts can be attacked.

    **Opponent discards** (`OpponentDiscards`) add to the opponent's pending discards, `PlayerState.to_discard`: public state, stacking one per effect. At the start of their next turn the opponent makes them, one `Discard(card)` each, a card of their choice from hand (the action names the card, like `PlayCard`). `EndTurn` caps the count at the next player's hand size, so discarding with an empty hand does nothing. **Decisions that must come first** live in one place, `rules._forced(state)`: while one is pending, its actions are the only legal ones and nothing is played automatically. Pending discards are the first; later effects that interrupt the turn with a decision belong there too. A dedicated `Discard` action fits better than a generic `Choose`: it names the card, as `PlayCard` and `Scrap` do, and `Choose` stays for the "or" choices.

    **Scrapping from hand or discard pile.** The rulebook lets a card's abilities be used "at any time during your Main Phase", so `ScrapHandOrDiscard` (Trade Bot, Missile Bot, Supply Bot, Battle Mech, Junkyard) doesn't interrupt the turn: it adds to `GameState.may_scrap`, the scraps the player may still make this turn (public, lost at the end of the turn). While it is positive, `legal_actions` offers one `ScrapHandCard(card)` per distinct card in hand and one `ScrapDiscardCard(card)` per distinct card in the discard pile, each using one; skipping is just not choosing one. Machine Base's `ScrapFromHand` ("draw a card, then scrap a card from your hand") is not optional and comes right after the draw, so it is a decision that must come first: it adds to `GameState.must_scrap` (if the hand isn't empty after the draw), and `_forced` then offers only one `ScrapHandCard` per distinct card in hand. Scrapped cards go to the scrap heap, Explorers back to their pile, without their scrap abilities. Two actions, not one with a "from" field, as the source matters when a card is in both piles, and each names the card like `Discard`.

    **Destroying a target base.** `DestroyBase` (Missile Mech's primary, Command Ship's ally ability, Port of Call's and Battlecruiser's scrap abilities) is optional ("you may destroy target base") and, like scrapping from hand or discard pile, usable at any time in the turn: it adds to `GameState.may_destroy` (public, lost at the end of the turn), and while that is positive `legal_actions` offers one `DestroyTarget(card)` per distinct base of the opponent's, Outposts or not (Outposts don't protect the others from it), each putting the base in its owner's discard pile, spending no combat, and using one. Skipping is just not choosing one. The opponent gains no bases during the player's turn, so with none in play the effect does nothing, and once the last one is destroyed (by `DestroyTarget` or `AttackBase`) any left over are dropped: the count stays 0 when it can't be used, so equal positions stay equal states for the transposition table. A dedicated action naming the base, like `AttackBase`; the effect is already called `DestroyBase`, so the action is `DestroyTarget`.

    **Scrapping from the Trade Row.** `ScrapTradeRow` (Battle Pod's primary, Blob Destroyer's ally ability: "you may scrap a card in the Trade Row") is optional and usable at any time in the turn, like the other "may" abilities: it adds to `GameState.may_scrap_row` (public, lost at the end of the turn), and while that is positive `legal_actions` offers one `ScrapTradeRowCard(card)` per distinct card in the row, each putting the card on the scrap heap, refilling its slot from the top of the Trade Deck exactly as `Acquire` does (`rules._take_from_row`), and using one. Skipping is just not choosing one. The row only empties once the Trade Deck has run out, and it never refills after that, so with it empty the effect does nothing and any scraps left over are dropped (as with `may_destroy`). The refill reveals a hidden card: views show the Trade Deck as a multiset, so the revealed card simply moves from it to the row, and a sampled world draws its next refill from the cards the view says are left. The transcript names the refill ("scraps Cutter from the Trade Row; Battle Pod replaces it"). A dedicated action naming the card, like `ScrapHandCard` and `ScrapDiscardCard`.
    **"Or" choices.** A `Choice` effect (Trading Post, Barter World, Defense Center, Patrol Mech, Blob World, Recycling Station, all in the primary ability) is resolved when the ability is used: it adds the card to `GameState.choosing` (public), and while that is non-empty `_forced` offers only one `Choose(card, option)` per option, in the card's order, naming the option's effects; choosing applies them. Choosing when the ability is used, not later in the turn, is the rule's reading ("gain 3 trade or 5 combat" happens as the ability resolves) and avoids branching on when to choose. Blob World's `DrawPerFactionCard` draws a card for each card of its faction played this turn: `GameState.factions_played` (public, reset at the end of the turn) counts each faction's cards played, ships and bases, whatever became of them since, so a Blob ship scrapped earlier still counts and Blob World counts itself when played this turn but not when used on a later turn. Recycling Station's `DiscardThenDraw` ("discard up to two cards, then draw that many") sets `GameState.recycling` (public) if the hand isn't empty, and `_forced` then offers one `Recycle(cards)` per distinct set of one or two cards from hand (sorted by name); the cards go to the discard pile and that many are drawn, so a reshuffle can bring them back. Discarding none isn't offered: it does nothing, and the trade option was better. `Recycle` names the cards, like `Discard`, and is its own action because it discards several at once and then draws.
    **Ships on top of the deck.** `NextShipOnTop` (Freighter's ally ability, Central Office's primary: "you may put the next ship you acquire this turn on top of your deck") is optional and, like the other "may" abilities, adds to `GameState.may_on_top` (public, lost at the end of the turn). While it is positive, `legal_actions` offers, beside each affordable ship's `Acquire` (Explorers included), an `AcquireOnTop(card)` that pays the same but puts the ship on top of the deck, using one. **A deliberate reading:** the player may put any one ship acquired later in the turn on top, not only the very next one. Read literally, declining for one ship would spend the ability, and a ship acquired before the ability is used is never eligible; then granting it early could be worse (a ship the player must buy first to reveal the one they want on top), so using it couldn't be automatic, and Central Office's trade would wait as a decision too. With this reading it is pure gain, every literal outcome stays reachable, and it costs only an extra action per affordable ship while it lasts. Each `NextShipOnTop` puts one ship on top, so Freighter's ally ability and Central Office together put two. `AcquireShipFreeOnTop` (Blob Carrier's ally ability: "acquire any ship without paying its cost and put it on top of your deck") is resolved when used, like an "or" choice: it adds to `GameState.free_ships` (if the Trade Row holds a ship; otherwise it does nothing), and while that is positive `_forced` offers only one `AcquireFreeShip(card)` per distinct ship in the Trade Row, which goes on top of the deck, its slot refilled as for `Acquire`. Explorers aren't offered: they aren't in the Trade Row, and one is never worth the free pick. It doesn't use `may_on_top` (it goes on top by its own effect). Dedicated actions naming the card, like `Acquire`. A card put on top of a deck is public (both players saw it acquired) until drawn: `PlayerState.known_top` counts the deck's known top cards, drawing uncovers them first, and the deck is only shuffled once empty, so a shuffle never hides one.
    **The one-off abilities.** Embassy Yacht's `DrawIfBases` ("if you have two or more bases in play, draw two cards") draws when it resolves if the player has that many bases in play then. Brain World's `ScrapThenDraw` ("scrap up to two cards from your hand and/or discard pile; draw a card for each card scrapped this way") is resolved when used, like recycling: it sets `GameState.scrap_to_draw` (public) if the hand or discard pile holds a card, and `_forced` then offers one `ScrapAndDraw(hand, discard)` per distinct choice of up to that many cards from the two piles, none included (each tuple sorted by name; scrapping none is the player's right, as "up to" says, and Brain World played from hand has no other option); the cards are scrapped (Explorers back to their pile), then that many are drawn. One action naming both piles' cards, like `Recycle`, because all are scrapped before the draw, and the source matters as for `ScrapHandCard` and `ScrapDiscardCard`. Fleet HQ's `ShipsGainCombat` ("all of your ships gain 1 combat") and Mech World's `AllyForAllFactions` ("counts as an ally for all factions") are always on while their base is in play: playing the base does nothing more, and there is no `UseBase` for them. **A deliberate reading:** Fleet HQ adds its combat to each ship played while it is in play (ships already in play when it arrives gain nothing), and Mech World is an ally for every faction's ally abilities but stays a Machine Cult card otherwise (Blob World doesn't count it as Blob). **Stealth Needle** ("copy another ship you've played this turn; Stealth Needle has that ship's faction in addition to Machine Cult") copies as it is played, since a ship's primary ability happens when it enters play: if another ship is in play, `CopyShip` sets `GameState.copying` (public) and `_forced` offers one `Copy(card)` per distinct other ship in play (with none, the Needle does nothing all turn). The copied ship's primary ability applies, and the Needle in play becomes a card of its own, `Stealth Needle (Ram)` say, with the target's primary, ally and scrap abilities and faction (`rules._copy`), and Machine Cult too for allies (`rules._allied`); `factions_played` counts it as a card of the target's faction too. Leaving play (end of turn, scrapped) it is Stealth Needle again (`rules.original`). **A deliberate reading:** the Needle copies a ship still in play, not one played and since scrapped: playing the Needle before scrapping that ship reaches the same outcomes, and the copy can't then be made of a card no longer anywhere in play. A dedicated action naming the ship, like `Acquire`.
  - `Effect`: `Trade`, `Combat`, `Authority`, `Draw`, `OpponentDiscards`, `DestroyBase`, `ScrapHandOrDiscard`, `ScrapTradeRow`, `NextShipOnTop`, `Choice`, plus one variant each for the base-set abilities that fit none of those: `AcquireShipFreeOnTop` (Blob Carrier), `DrawIfBases` (Embassy Yacht), `DrawPerFactionCard` (Blob World), `DiscardThenDraw` (Recycling Station), `ScrapThenDraw` (Brain World), `ScrapFromHand` (Machine Base), `CopyShip` (Stealth Needle), `ShipsGainCombat` (Fleet HQ) and `AllyForAllFactions` (Mech World). Each is implemented in the slice that first needs its card.

  Cards are plain frozen dataclasses loaded from the data file.
- **Moves are micro-actions, with conservative automatic play.** Actions that can't make any later option worse are applied automatically and never branch. Everything else is a decision node: buys, scraps, targets, "or" choices, attack allocation, and orderings that interact with draws. Baselines and search bots share the same move generator.

  The rule lives in one place, `rules.automatic(state, action)`. An action is automatic when it is:
  - `PlayCard` of a **ship** whose primary effects are all pure gain (trade, combat, Authority, making the opponent discard, letting the player scrap from hand or discard pile or from the Trade Row, letting them destroy one of the opponent's bases, or letting them put a ship they acquire on top of their deck), **unless something could scrap that ship from hand this turn instead**: a scrap the player may still make (`may_scrap`), another card in hand that scraps from hand when played (Trade Bot, Machine Base, ...), or a base's unused primary or a card's unused ally ability in play that does (`rules._hand_scrap_ahead`). Hand and ships in play both go to the discard pile at the end of the turn, and in play the ship only adds options (ally abilities, scrapping), but a played ship can no longer be scrapped from hand. Making the opponent discard only adds to the discards they make at the start of their next turn, and a scrap or a base destruction the player may make waits for them, so nothing in this turn depends on when either happens (the opponent's bases only go during the turn, so granting a destroy early is never worse: a Missile Mech played after the last base fell destroys nothing; likewise the Trade Row only changes when the player buys or scraps from it, so granting a row scrap early is never worse, and Battle Pod and Blob Destroyer's ally ability are automatic; a ship may go on top whenever it is acquired after the ability is used, so granting that early is never worse either, and Freighter's ally ability and Central Office on the turns after it is played are automatic). So a Trade Bot is played automatically, and then the Scouts and Vipers in hand wait as decisions until the scrap is made. One accepted gap: a draw may bring a card that scraps from hand after a ship was played automatically; counting the deck's scrappers too would stop automatic play whenever a draw is possible in a Machine Cult deck. **An "or" choice between pure-gain options counts as pure gain** (Patrol Mech's 3 trade or 5 combat): playing the ship (or using such a base, Trading Post, Barter World or Defense Center, on a later turn) is automatic, and the `Choose` it leaves is the decision. Either option gives the same whenever it is chosen, so every outcome stays reachable; the second accepted gap is information: a view-based bot chooses before any draws later in the turn, which it accepts rather than branching on when to use the ability. A choice with a draw among its options (Blob World, Recycling Station) is not pure gain. **Embassy Yacht** counts as pure gain while the player can't have the two bases its draw needs this turn (bases in play plus bases in hand fall short; `rules._cannot_draw`): its draw can't happen, so it is trade and Authority; otherwise it is a decision like any draw. The third accepted gap: a draw may bring a base that would have let it draw. **Ships wait in hand while a Fleet HQ is there too** (`rules._bonus_ahead`): played after the HQ, each gains 1 combat. **Stealth Needle in hand keeps ships waiting** as a hand scrapper would, while a ship in play scraps from hand with its primary or ally ability (the Needle could copy it), and **Brain World** counts as a scrapper from hand like Junkyard;
  - `UseAlly` whose ally effects are all pure gain;
  - `UseBase` whose primary effects are all pure gain.

  Not automatic: `Discard` (the discarding player's choice of card); anything with `Draw` (a draw can reshuffle the discard pile, and acquired cards go there, so drawing before or after a buy changes the deck); **playing a base**, even a pure-gain one (a base left in hand goes to the discard pile and is reshuffled, played it stays out of the deck, so it changes deck composition); `Scrap` (Survey Ship's scrap ability, making the opponent discard, still loses the card), `ScrapHandCard`, `ScrapDiscardCard` and `ScrapTradeRowCard` (the choice of card), `DestroyTarget` (the choice of base), `Choose` (the choice of option), `Recycle` and `ScrapAndDraw` (the choice of cards), playing Stealth Needle and `Copy` (the choice of ship), using Brain World (it draws), `Acquire`, `AcquireOnTop`, `AcquireFreeShip` (the choice of ship), Blob Carrier's ally ability (the Trade Row it takes from changes as the player buys and scraps, so when to use it matters), attacks and `EndTurn`. Any new effect or action must revisit the rule.

  `rules.auto_play(state)` applies automatic actions, in `legal_actions` order, until none is left; `rules.advance(state, action)` applies a decision then auto-plays. `rules.advance_legal` is the same for an action taken from `legal_actions(state)`: it skips `apply`'s legality check (which recomputes `legal_actions`), so the search uses it, while bots' actions go through `advance` and are checked. The match runner auto-plays after dealing and after every action, so bots are only offered decisions. `rules.apply` stays the bare rules, without automatic play. Tests brute-force every way to play a turn from small hand-built states with and without automatic play: every outcome with it is reachable without, and every outcome without it is matched by one with it that leaves the player at least as much Authority, the opponent at most as much and at least as many discards to make (a pure-gain card left unplayed only loses Authority, damage or discards). Two of the states scrap from hand or discard pile (a Trade Bot, and Machine Base's forced scrap), one destroys a base (Missile Mech and Command Ship's ally ability, beside a base combat could also destroy), one scraps from the Trade Row (Battle Pod, around buys before and after the refill), one makes "or" choices (Patrol Mech and Defense Center automatically, a Trading Post in hand as a decision, around a buy and a draw), one puts ships on top of the deck (Freighter's ally ability and Central Office's, around buys before and after a refill, an Explorer, and Central Office's ally draw of a ship put on top), one takes Blob Carrier's free ship, and the last slice's play Embassy Yacht (with a base in play, and with another in hand), Fleet HQ in hand, Mech World's allies, Stealth Needle's copies (one with Trade Bot to copy) and Brain World's scraps; tests check the comparison catches automatic play that ignores scrapping from hand (Stealth Needle's included), Fleet HQ in hand, or a base in hand that lets Embassy Yacht draw.
- **Fully specified worlds.** The state always holds a concrete order for every deck, driven by a seeded RNG, so a seed fully determines a game.
- **Observation views.** `observe(state, player)` returns what that player knows: their own hand, everything public (pending discards, scraps, bases to destroy, Trade Row scraps, choices and recycling being made, the factions played this turn, ships left to put on top of the deck, free ships owed, and Brain World's scraps and Stealth Needle's copy being made among it), and the contents but not the order of hidden piles, except the cards known to be on top of a deck (`PlayerView.deck_top`, top first, left out of `unseen`). **Bots only ever receive views**, enforced by tests. The one exception is the benchmark-only "cheat" bot: a bot marks itself benchmark-only by implementing `bots.BenchmarkBot` (`choose_from_state(state, actions)`) instead of `bots.Bot` (`choose(view, actions)`), and only then does the match runner give it the true state. The tournament's timing wrapper keeps that marking.
- **Sampling.** A sampler builds a full world consistent with a view, used by the sampler bot and ISMCTS. `view.sample_world(view, rng)` takes a `random.Random`: it shuffles each player's `unseen` cards, deals enough to fill the hand to `hand_size` (none to the observer, whose hand is known) and the rest to the deck under its known top cards, shuffles the Trade Deck, and draws a fresh 64-bit seed for future shuffles, so `observe(sample_world(v, rng), v.observer) == v`.

## Search

- **Depth counts turns, not plies.** `alphabeta` gains a generic `ply_cost: Callable[[N], int]` hook, defaulting to 1 per node so other games are unchanged. Star Realms passes 1 at the end of a turn and 0 otherwise, so the search only stops at turn boundaries. The default horizon is 2 turns: my turn plus the opponent's reply.
- **Transpositions are merged.** `alphabeta` takes an optional generic `key: Callable[[N], Hashable]` hook (default `None`: no table, so other games are unchanged). With it, results are stored by key and remaining depth, with whether each is exact or a fail-soft lower or upper bound, and reused only where valid for the current window. A reused leaf's `moves` are rebuilt from the current path, so callers can still read the principal line from the returned leaf. A `table` argument lets searches share one (for iterative deepening).
- **Evaluation**: `evaluate(state, perspective) -> int`, where bigger is better for `perspective`. It starts as a hand-written linear function of:
  - the Authority difference;
  - base defense in play, with Outposts weighted higher;
  - deck economy: the average **card value** over every card the player owns (deck, hand, in play, discard and bases). A card's value counts everything it does: its primary abilities, its ally abilities times the chance one fires (`1 - (1 - share)^4`, where `share` is the fraction of the player's other cards of its faction, and 4 is the rest of a hand), and its scrap abilities at a quarter (used once, and they cost the card). An ability is worth the sum of its effects, each valued per effect kind (trade, combat, Authority, draws, opponent discards, scraps, destroying a base, Trade Row scraps, the next ship on top, a free ship, copying, Fleet HQ's bonus, Mech World's allies, ...; an exhaustive `match`, so a new `Effect` variant must be given a value), and a choice is worth its best option. The values are in `evaluate.Weights` with the other weights, chosen by hand (#114); tuning them by tournament (#115, the `tune` command) found no change that beats the noise, so they stand (results, Milestone 5: tuning). Each card's value is precomputed once per `Weights`, keyed by name, since `evaluate` is in the search's hot path;
  - a penalty per Scout/Viper still owned.

  The paragraphs below are about state *during* a turn, which the card values don't change: they value the cards a player owns, not the abilities being resolved.

  Pending discards aren't scored (Milestone 4 left `evaluate` alone): a 2-turn search sees them made in the opponent's turn, and the plays that cause them are automatic, except scrapping a Survey Ship, which a 1-turn search undervalues.

  Bases the player may still destroy (`may_destroy`) aren't scored either (the destroy-base slice left `evaluate` alone): like scraps, they are lost at the end of the turn and the search sees them used within it, and the base-defense term already scores the destroyed base.

  Trade Row scraps the player may still make (`may_scrap_row`) aren't scored either (the Trade Row slice left `evaluate` alone): lost at the end of the turn, used within it, and `evaluate` doesn't score the Trade Row.

  Choices being made (`choosing`, `recycling`) and the factions played (`factions_played`) aren't scored either (the "or" choices slice left `evaluate` alone): the choice is made at once, before anything else, and the count only matters to Blob World within the turn.

  Ships the player may still put on top of their deck (`may_on_top`) and free ships owed (`free_ships`) aren't scored either (the next-ship-on-top slice left `evaluate` alone): lost at the end of the turn or resolved at once, and `evaluate` already counts every card the player owns, wherever it is.

  Brain World's scraps and Stealth Needle's copy being made (`scrap_to_draw`, `copying`) aren't scored either (the last slice left `evaluate` alone): both are resolved at once, before anything else. Fleet HQ's bonus and Mech World's allies are valued as their cards' effects in the card values (#114), not by whether the base is in play.

  Scraps the player may still make (`may_scrap`) aren't scored either (the scrap-from-hand slice left `evaluate` alone too): they are lost at the end of the turn, and the search sees them made within it; the starter-card penalty already rewards scrapping Scouts and Vipers.

  Each term is scored for both players and differenced, so the score is antisymmetric. A finished game scores `WIN` (or `-WIN`), and other scores are capped strictly inside that. The weights live in one place (`evaluate.Weights`, default `WEIGHTS`) and are tuned by tournament: `tune` (`python -m minimax_games.games.star_realms tune`, README, Development) runs a paired coordinate search of the cheat, given candidate weights (`CheatBot(weights=...)`), against the heuristic. The searches take the evaluation as a parameter, an `evaluate.Evaluator` (`evaluator(state, perspective) -> int`, plus `describe()` for the settings line): `evaluate.Linear(weights)`, the default `LINEAR`, or **rollouts**, `rollout.Rollout(rollouts=1, turns=2, epsilon=0)`: each playout plays `turns` more turn ends with the greedy bot's moves read from the state (`rollout.greedy`: only the legal actions and the scraps owed, so no hidden cards and no view), each move replaced with probability `epsilon` by a random one other than `EndTurn`, and scores the state reached as 1 or 0 if the game ended, else `sigmoid(evaluate / scale)` (`scale` 16, fitted to heuristic-vs-heuristic games); the mean is mapped to `-SPAN..SPAN` (`SPAN = 10**6`), inside `-WIN..WIN`. Its rng is seeded from its `seed`, the perspective and the state's shuffle seed and turn, so a state scores the same whatever the search order or process. `Rollout.value(state, perspective, rng)` serves searches other than alpha-beta. At equal compute (700 nodes with rollouts against 5,000 linear, `rollout.ROLLOUT_BUDGET`) and at four times it, rollouts did not beat the linear evaluation, so linear stays the default (results, Milestone 5: rollouts). A learned value is the third, `value.ValueEvaluator` (Learned values, below).
- **Bots**, in the order they're built:
  1. **cheat**: alpha-beta on the true world. It's a benchmark and upper bound, not a legitimate player. `node.StarRealmsNode` adapts a state to `search.Node` from the searching player's side: children from `legal_actions` + `rules.advance_legal`, `score()` from `evaluate` (a finished game's `WIN` moved one point toward 0 per move, so quicker wins and slower losses rank higher), and `node.ends_turn` as the `ply_cost`. `CheatBot(turns=2)` plays the first move of the best line; it is `cheat` in the CLI. The search uses `alphabeta`'s transposition table (its generic `key` hook, here `node.transposition_key`: the state and the number of moves to it, since a finished game's score depends on that), so the many orderings of a turn's micro-actions that reach the same state are searched once. Each decision has a deterministic node budget (`CheatBot(budget=5_000)` by default, `cheat.BUDGET`, the default total budget the cheat and the sampler share: re-measured after the optimisation pass, a cheat game against a baseline takes about 4.5 s and a 500-game search-bot tournament about half an hour with `--jobs 3`; see [star-realms-results.md](star-realms-results.md), Milestone 3): `cheat.decide` deepens one turn at a time, 1 then 2 up to the horizon, and plays the best move of the deepest search that finished within the budget, or, if not even the 1-turn search finished, the best of the root moves it finished (the first legal action if none). Nodes are charged as they are generated by a `StarRealmsNode` subclass that raises once the budget is spent, so `alphabeta` is unchanged and a decision never passes its budget. The tournament command prints the cheat's horizon and budget.
  2. **sampler (PIMC)**: sample K worlds from the bot's view, run depth-limited alpha-beta on each, and vote on the move. `sampler.SamplerBot(seed, worlds=4, turns=2, budget=cheat.BUDGET)` (4 worlds by default, `sampler.WORLDS`, chosen in Milestone 3 of the results) is an ordinary `Bot` (views only): each decision (`sampler.vote`) samples `worlds` worlds with `view.sample_world`, from an rng seeded with the bot's seed so a game replays identically, runs `cheat.decide` on each with `budget // worlds` nodes, and plays the move most worlds chose, ties broken in `legal_actions` order. The total budget defaults to the cheat's, so the two compare at equal compute. It is `sampler` in the CLI. Search bots state their settings (`bots.Budgeted.settings()`), which the tournament command prints: the cheat's horizon and budget, and the sampler's worlds too. `tournament --budget N --worlds K` overrides the search bots' total budget and the sampler's worlds. `--eval rollout` (with `--rollouts`, `--rollout-turns`, `--epsilon`, seeded per game, and `ROLLOUT_BUDGET` nodes unless `--budget` is given) gives both search bots the rollout evaluator, which their settings then state.
  3. **ISMCTS**: single-observer information-set MCTS with availability counts (Cowling, Powley and Whitehouse 2012), designed in [ismcts.md](ismcts.md). The generic search is `search.ismcts.ismcts(game, sample, iterations, rng, exploration=0.7)`, two-player and zero-sum like `alphabeta`: the game supplies legal actions (in a fixed order), apply, the player to move, terminal, and player 0's leaf value in [0, 1] (`search.ismcts.Game`); `sample()` returns a fresh world consistent with the root information set every iteration. Nodes are identified by the action path from the root (every Star Realms action is public, so that path is the information set), no states are stored in the tree, and chance is not branched on ("open loop"). Selection is UCB1 over the actions legal in the iteration's world, each child's availability count standing in for the parent's visits; one random untried legal action is expanded per iteration; the move is the most visited root action (ties by mean reward, then `legal_actions` order). `ismcts.IsmctsBot(seed, iterations=450, exploration=0.7, epsilon=0.25, extra_turns=0)` is an ordinary `Bot` (views only): micro-actions are edges, `rules.advance_legal` (automatic play inside the edge), `EndTurn` leads into the opponent's turn, and a leaf is valued by finishing the turn in progress (and `extra_turns` more) with `rollout.playout`'s epsilon-greedy policy, then `sigmoid(evaluate / 16)` for player 0 (`rollout.playout`, `rollout.win_probability`; #116's fitted scale). Options measured and left off: `policy="heuristic"` (the heuristic bot's moves from the mover's view, `rollout.heuristic`) and `horizon` (every leaf played out to the end of the same turn from the root). A lone legal action is played without searching. Each decision draws separate seeds for its worlds and its playouts from the bot's rng, so a game replays identically. 450 iterations is about the sampler's time per decision on CPython. At that budget it plays level with the sampler (34.6% against the heuristic to the sampler's 33.4% on the same seeds, 48.6% head to head), and 4x the iterations gained nothing (results, Milestone 5: ISMCTS). It is `ismcts` in the CLI; `tournament --iterations N` sets its budget.
  4. **planner**: the whole-turn planner (below), `planner.PlannerBot(seed, worlds=4, budget=20_000, evaluator=LINEAR)`, an ordinary `Bot` (views only). It is `planner` in the CLI, and `planner:<model>` (e.g. `planner:mlp`) is the planner scoring with a committed learned value (`value.load(<model>)`) on the Rust enumeration; `tournament --budget N --worlds K` set its states per plan and worlds (`--eval rollout` gives it the rollout evaluator but leaves its budget, which is in states, not alpha-beta nodes), and the tournament reports its plans per turn and time per plan (`bots.Planning`: a bot that counts its `plans` and `turns`; `tournament.Timing.plans_per_turn`, `ms_per_plan`, all its time per decision over its plans).
- **Whole-turn planner** (`planner.py`, milestone 6): chooses moves by scoring end-of-turn states with a value function, any `evaluate.Evaluator`: the hand-valued `evaluate` by default, a learned one (`value.ValueEvaluator`) later. An end of turn is the state as `EndTurn` leaves it, before the next player's automatic play (`rules.apply_legal`), the same boundary the learned values are fitted at (automatic play would reveal the next player's hand in a sampled world), or the state after a play that wins. A plan (`planner.plan(worlds, budget, evaluator)`) samples N worlds from the view (`view.sample_world`). In each world it lists the distinct end-of-turn states reachable from the current state, merging orderings through a transposition table, and scores each with the value. A **chance event** is a play that draws a card no one knows (not one known to be on top of the deck), reshuffles the discard pile, or reveals a card from the Trade Deck (`planner.reveals`: the rng seed, the Trade Deck's size or the unknown part of the mover's deck changes; a test checks that a play it calls no chance event leaves every sampled world with the same view). Each candidate line up to the next chance event or the end of the turn (the same in every world, since nothing hidden is revealed along it) is worth the mean, over worlds, of the best end of turn reachable through it. The planner plays the best line up to the next draw or Trade Row reveal and plans again only then: between chance events nothing new is learned, so planning again would only find the same line, and it never commits past a chance event on what the sampled worlds say. That makes one to three plans per turn, not one per decision. This is exact when one chance event is left in the turn, because the player sees the drawn or revealed card before choosing what follows, so the best completion *given* that card is what they will actually play. With more chance events left, a world's completion after the first already knows the later ones, which biases the value of draw-heavy lines upward (strategy fusion, limited to one turn). Every candidate is scored at the same boundary, the end of the player's own turn, so a bias that depends only on who moves next (being the side to move is worth about 6 `evaluate` points) is the same for all of them and can't change the choice. With one world and no chance event in the turn, its line reaches the best end of turn of a brute-force walk of every ordering (a test checks it on seeded turns). **The line is followed while the game goes as planned**: before each of its moves the bot checks that its view, up to pile order (`planner.seen`), is the one the line reached in the first world, and plans again if not (`PlannerBot.diverged` counts those plans). Views don't keep the order of a hand, and automatic play plays cards in hand order, which can decide whether a card is played automatically or waits as a decision (a ship played before a card in play that could scrap it from hand, `rules._hand_scrap_ahead`, or a choice made at once that stops automatic play until it is made). So a sampled world, its hand sorted by name, can meet a decision the real hand order doesn't offer, or miss one it does; nothing hidden is involved, and planning again from the view costs only time.

  Its budget is deterministic, like the other bots': a number of distinct states visited per plan, ends and states within the turn, shared by the worlds. The part of the turn before the first chance event is the same in every world, so it is enumerated once, in the first world, with at most half the budget; each line that leaves it is a candidate, replayed in the other worlds. The rest of the budget is split evenly among the worlds, and each lists the ends through each candidate in turn, depth first, the greedy policy's preferred move first (`bots.greedy_move`), so the greedy line is the first candidate and the first end found. Where a world's share runs out, the candidate it was on is worth the best end found through it so far and later candidates aren't reached in that world; the candidates reached in the most worlds compete (ties to the first found), and if none was reached the planner plays the greedy move and plans again at the next decision. The worlds are drawn from an rng seeded with the bot's seed, so a game replays identically. The enumeration has two backends (`backend=`): the Python one, the reference, which scores each end as it finds it with any `Evaluator`, and the Rust one (#140; Compiled engine), which lists the same ends in the same order, returns their feature rows as one matrix, and needs a learned value that scores rows (`value.ValueEvaluator.score_rows`); the plans, and so the games, are identical, about 14 times faster. The default is **20,000 states per plan and 4 worlds** (`planner.BUDGET`, `planner.WORLDS`), from the turn-size measurement (results, Milestone 6: full-deck turn sizes): 20,000 lists every end of 88% of turns for about 1 s on CPython, but 30-50% of turns from turn 21 on pass it.

  **States that differ only in pile order are merged**, ends and states within the turn alike: the key (`planner.merge_key`, equal exactly when `turn_sizes.unordered` is, a test checks; a flat tuple a quarter of `unordered`'s cost) sorts every pile but the decks (hand, in play, discard pile, bases, Trade Row, scrap heap, abilities used). About two thirds of distinct ends differ only in such an order, almost all the discard pile's, from playing the same cards in another order. Nothing in the rules reads a pile's order except a reshuffle of the discard pile, and `rules._shuffle` permutes positions by draws from `random.Random(seed)` that depend only on the seed and the pile's size, never on its cards, and returns the same next seed whatever the order. Each sampled world has a fresh seed from `sample_world`, so the deck a reshuffle makes has the same distribution over the worlds whatever order the discard pile was in. The player can't see the seed, so they can't choose a shuffle by choosing an order: the orders are one decision. Keeping them apart would only let a world pick the order that happens to shuffle best in it (a small clairvoyance no player has), and the value can't tell them apart anyway, since `evaluate` and the learned values' features are order-invariant. On the turns of 4 seeded heuristic games, 4 worlds and the default budget, merging visits a third fewer states and finishes 87 of 100 plans instead of 78, at the same time per plan. Canonical orderings of actions that commute (a scrap from the discard pile commutes with everything but a draw or a buy) are the next exact saving if the budget still binds too often; approximate search within the turn (evolutionary planning, or MCTS with ends of turn as leaves) is for turns neither can shrink. Pruning mid-turn states by the value function is ruled out: the value is only meant to be accurate at turn boundaries.
- **Deterministic budgets**: worlds × turn depth for the sampler, iterations for ISMCTS, states per plan for the planner, and a number of rollouts. No wall-clock limits in anything we measure. Bots are compared at equal budgets.
- **Performance**: benchmark nodes per second once the cheat works. `bench` (`python -m minimax_games.games.star_realms bench`, or `task bench`) runs the cheat's search (`cheat.search`) on fixed positions, each the start of a turn in a seeded greedy-vs-greedy game (`bench.position(seed, turn)`), at horizons 1 and 2 turns. Nodes are counted by a `StarRealmsNode` subclass, so `alphabeta` is unchanged, and each search stops past a deterministic node budget (`--max-nodes`), so node counts are reproducible and the command always finishes; only timings vary. `--states` also counts distinct states, in each search and exactly over each position's whole first turn (`bench.turn_tree`, which merges transpositions), to size the transposition problem. `--budget N` times the cheat's budgeted decisions (`cheat.decide`) at the same positions instead: the depth each finished, its nodes and its time. With `--worlds K` too, it times the sampler's decisions (`sampler.vote`, K worlds sampled from the mover's view sharing the N nodes) instead: the depth each world's search finished (`Vote.depths`), the nodes and the time. `turn-sizes` (`python -m minimax_games.games.star_realms turn-sizes`, or `task turn-sizes`) measures the whole-turn planner's workload: for every turn of `--games` seeded heuristic-vs-heuristic games (`turn_sizes.turn_starts`), the distinct end-of-turn states, the same up to the order of every pile but the decks (`turn_sizes.unordered`), and the distinct within-turn states visited to list them (`turn_sizes.turn_ends`, which merges orderings as `bench.turn_tree` does, checked against brute-force enumeration in the tests), each turn stopped past `--max-states` states and counted as capped; it prints their distribution, the share of turns over candidate state budgets, and both by turn number, and is deterministic apart from timings. Results are in [star-realms-results.md](star-realms-results.md). The target is a 500-game tournament between two search bots in about 10 minutes on a laptop. Optimise the state representation only if the benchmark says search depth limits strength.

## Compiled engine (#127; the engine plays the full deck, #137 and #138)

Learning a value by policy iteration needs tens of thousands of self-play games per round, each with the whole-turn planner choosing every move. In Python that is days of CPU per round, so the engine is compiled, in **Rust** (PyO3 and maturin), and Python keeps orchestration.

Orchestration is split by granularity: **Python orchestrates per round, Rust per move.**

- **Rust:** game state, legal actions, applying actions with automatic play, `observe` and `sample_world`, the planner (for N sampled worlds, the distinct end-of-turn states of the current turn, merged through a transposition table, built in #140; then the choice among them, built in #141), the feature encoder (one row of order-invariant features per end state, built in #140), the value's forward pass (hand-written: a dot product for a logistic, a few matrix products for a small network, built in #141), the heuristic bot (built, #139), and playing whole games across threads: given weights, seeds and opponents, it returns the training rows and results (built in #141).
- **Python:** which games to play with which weights, training, gating and tournaments, statistics, tuning, the CLI and transcripts. It also keeps a planner that scores each plan's feature matrix in one numpy call: that is where a new form of value is tried on a small budget, before it is worth writing its forward pass in Rust.
- **The Python engine stays the reference.** The Rust engine, planner and heuristic are checked against the Python ones by differential tests: seeded games with deterministic bots, comparing states, legal actions, views and choices after every action.

Why the split falls there: crossing into Python once per plan costs microseconds against roughly a millisecond of enumeration, so a single game is fine with Python scoring. Self-play is not: many games on many threads, each calling back into Python for every plan, would queue on Python's global interpreter lock, and Python would set the pace. With the forward pass in Rust, a policy-iteration round is one call from Python. Crossing per micro-action, as a Python search over a Rust `advance` would, is ruled out for the same reason. The heuristic moves to Rust because it is an opponent in every self-play round, and a Python opponent would put Python back in the loop. numpy is a core dependency: it does the training's matrix arithmetic, the Python planner's scoring, and carries feature matrices across the boundary without copying.

Why Rust: the planner's inner loop hashes states into a table, which Rust does natively with value types, and the rules' intricate cases (choices, copies, forced decisions) stay checked by exhaustive `match` on enums, the same discipline as superenums in Python. Numba suits batched array stepping but not this workload: hash tables of states need its experimental typed `Dict` and hand-flattened states, and its kernels lose exhaustiveness checking.

Layout: one crate (`rust/`) builds one extension module, `minimax_games._native`, with Star Realms as its module, in maturin's mixed layout, so the Python package stays at `src/minimax_games/`. The repo may grow other Rust engines; the crate becomes a Cargo workspace when a second one arrives, not before. maturin replaces `uv_build` as the build backend, and the Rust toolchain is pinned in `rust-toolchain.toml`, as uv is pinned by `required-version`. Rust's checks (`cargo fmt --check`, `cargo clippy -- -D warnings`, `cargo test`) are pre-commit hooks, so CI stays the one required `pre-commit` job. Releases ship an sdist and a macOS arm64 wheel.

The port is sliced like the Python engine: the first slice's cards first, as the tracer bullet that also sets up the toolchain (maturin, cargo test, clippy and rustfmt in pre-commit, Rust in CI), then the remaining kinds of effect. Its speed is measured in absolute terms, time per planner decision and per self-play game, not against CPython, which is known to be too slow. Effort on the engine is watched, because the engine serves the goal and isn't the goal: each slice reports the time it took and the bugs differential testing caught.

**The tracer bullet (#137), as built.** Results: Milestone 7 in [star-realms-results.md](star-realms-results.md).

- **Toolchain.** `rust-toolchain.toml` at the repo root pins Rust (1.95.0, with clippy and rustfmt); it is at the root, not in `rust/`, because rustup finds it from the working directory and everything runs from the root. maturin is the build backend (`[tool.maturin]`: `manifest-path = "rust/Cargo.toml"`, `module-name = "minimax_games._native"`, `python-source = "src"`), so `uv sync` compiles the extension (in release) into `src/minimax_games/`, where git ignores it. `[tool.uv] cache-keys` lists the Rust files, so `uv run` rebuilds the extension whenever they change and the tests never run a stale build. The crate's PyO3 code is behind its `python` feature, which maturin turns on: `cargo test` builds plain Rust with no Python to link, and clippy checks everything (`--all-features`, run through `uv run` so PyO3's build script finds the venv's Python). The extension uses the stable ABI from 3.13 (`abi3-py313`), so one macOS arm64 wheel serves every supported Python.
- **Checks.** `cargo fmt --check`, `cargo clippy -- -D warnings` and `cargo test` are pre-commit hooks, and pytest also runs on Rust and TOML changes, so the differential tests check every Rust change. CI's one `pre-commit` job installs the pinned toolchain (`rustup toolchain install`) and caches cargo (`Swatinem/rust-cache`) before `uv sync`. The release workflow runs on an Apple-silicon runner and builds the sdist (which carries `rust-toolchain.toml`) and the wheel on every PR as its dry run, and on `main` when it releases.
- **Engine** (`rust/src/star_realms/`): `card.rs` (the cards as an enum, with their data in an exhaustive `match`), `state.rs`, `rules.rs` (dealing, legal actions, `advance` with automatic play), `bots.rs` (the greedy bot and a whole greedy game) and `rng.rs`. Piles are `Vec<Card>` (inline lists since #156) in the Python tuples' order, so states compare field by field; the Python fields the first slice never sets are left out, and later slices add them with their effects. Actions are a Rust enum of the first slice's variants (`PlayCard`, `UseAlly`, `UseBase`, `Scrap`, `Acquire`, `AttackPlayer`, `AttackBase`, `EndTurn`), and every `match` over actions and effects is exhaustive, so a new variant fails to compile until every rule handles it. The card data is written in Rust, not read from `cards.toml`; a differential test checks it against the Python catalogue, card by card.
- **Seeds deal bit-identical games.** `rng.rs` reimplements CPython's `random` as far as the engine uses it: MT19937, seeding from an integer (`init_by_array` over its 32-bit words), `getrandbits`, `_randbelow` and `sample(cards, len(cards))` (always CPython's pool branch when sampling every card). It is about 150 lines, checked by `cargo test` against values from CPython, and it makes the differential tests exact: a seed deals the same game in both engines, so every state and every list of legal actions must be equal, not just statistically alike. The alternatives were weaker and no cheaper: statistical equivalence can't localise a bug to an action, and replaying Python-recorded shuffles would need the Python engine to run beside every Rust game. The cost is that the Rust engine's shuffles stay tied to CPython's algorithms, which have been stable for many releases; if one changes, the `cargo test` vectors fail first.
- **Boundary**: `minimax_games._native.star_realms` (`rust/src/python.rs`, stubs in `src/minimax_games/_native/`): `Game(seed)` deals and auto-plays a first-slice game, with `legal_actions()`, `advance(kind, card)`, `state()`, `winner()` and `greedy_move()`; actions cross as `(kind, card name or None)` pairs named as the Python variants, states as dicts of card names. `play_greedy(seed)` plays a whole greedy game in Rust; `bench_games` and `bench_advance` time it (`task native-bench`). The stubs are a directory of `.pyi` files beside the compiled `_native`: without an `__init__.py` it is only a namespace package, so the compiled module wins on import. The submodule registers itself in `sys.modules`, so `import minimax_games._native.star_realms` works.
- **Differential tests** (`tests/star_realms/test_native.py`): 200 greedy-vs-greedy and 100 seeded random first-slice games, comparing the state and the legal actions after every action, the Rust greedy bot's choice at every decision, and its whole-game result. (Superseded by the full-deck tests below.)

**The full deck (#138), as built.** The engine plays the whole 80-card base set, every kind of effect and action the Python engine has, plus `observe` and `sample_world`. Results: Milestone 7 (full deck) in [star-realms-results.md](star-realms-results.md).

The random games must take every kind of action at least once, so the tests can't quietly stop covering one. Another test checks that a sampled world has the view it was sampled from. **The heuristic bot (#139), as built.** Results: Milestone 7 (heuristic bot) in [star-realms-results.md](star-realms-results.md).

- **Bot** (`heuristic.rs`): `heuristic_move(view, actions)` is `bots.HeuristicBot.choose`, step for step in its priority order, with the same module constants. It reads only the view and the legal actions, as the Python bot does; `play_heuristic(seed, max_turns)` plays a whole heuristic-vs-heuristic game, observing the current player's view before every decision.
- **Floats match Python's exactly.** The scores `worth` and `keep` are `f64`, computed with Python's operations in Python's order (the cost, then the synergy, the base defense and the trade or combat terms, each added in turn), and sums over cards fold from 0 in the list's order, as `sum` does. Every term is a multiple of 0.5 far inside `f64`'s exact range, so every sum is exact whatever the order (Python's compensated float `sum` gives the same) and the one rounded operation, the average worth of the Trade Deck in the Trade Row scrap, is the same IEEE division in both. Ties go to the first least key, as Python's `min` does: keys are tuples compared with `PartialOrd` (`first_min`), never NaN, names compare as Python's strings (byte order is code-point order).
- **Boundary**: `Game.heuristic_move()`, the heuristic's choice from the current player's view; `play_heuristic(seed)`, a whole game, `(winner, turns, decisions)`; `bench_heuristic_games`, timed by `task native-bench`.
- **Differential tests** (`tests/star_realms/test_native.py`): 60 heuristic-vs-heuristic, 60 heuristic-vs-greedy and 60 heuristic-vs-random full-deck games, seats alternating with the seed. At every decision, whoever's it is, the Rust heuristic must choose what the Python one chooses from the current player's view, so the random games put it in rare positions (recycling, Brain World, free ships) the heuristic seldom reaches itself; and `play_heuristic` must give the Python game's winner and turns. It fails at the first divergence, naming the seed, the action number and the turn, and must see the heuristic choose every kind of action. About 2 s.

**The planner's enumeration and the feature encoder (#140), as built.** Results: Milestone 7 (planner enumeration) in [star-realms-results.md](star-realms-results.md).

- **Enumeration** (`planner.rs`): `enumerate(worlds, budget)` is `planner.plan` up to the scoring, walk for walk: the shared part before the first chance event listed once in the first world with at most half the budget (`reveals` as Python's), the rest split evenly among the worlds, each listing the ends through each candidate in turn, depth first, the greedy move first, and stopping at its share. Merging uses the whole state with every pile but the decks (and the pending choices) sorted as its key (`merge_key`: sorting by the card enum's order gives the same equivalence as Python's sort by name), in `rustc-hash` tables, which hash a state card by card several times faster than the standard SipHash. (#156 replaced the sorted copies with tables of indices: "Fewer copies" below.) The enumeration never reads a value: what Python's walk takes the best of is fixed by which ends it reaches, so Rust records the graph of plays between the states each world lists and, per world and candidate, the ends whose best is the candidate's value there: the ends found through it and every end below a state it reuses (including those of a candidate cut short by the budget, which Python values at its best end found so far). The ends come out in the order the Python planner scores them, world after world.
- **Encoder** (`features.rs`): `features.encode`'s columns and arithmetic, into one flat `f64` buffer per plan; the counts are small integers, so the rows are bit-identical to Python's. A catalogue card's column is its place in `Card::ALL`, found by binary search (the enum's derived order is the catalogue's, a test checks).
- **Shuffles are cached** (`rules::shuffle`): a shuffle takes cards by positions drawn from the seed alone, so the positions and the next seed are kept per seed and pile size (a thread-local table of up to 256, cleared when full). Most ends of a turn reshuffle the same world's discard pile from the same seed, and seeding the Mersenne Twister had been a fifth of a plan's time.
- **Boundary**: `plan_ends(worlds, budget, ends=False)` takes the worlds as state dicts (`planner.native_state`) and returns a dict: `features`, the rows as one float64 numpy matrix handed over without a copy (rust-numpy), with each row's `world`, `winner` and `current`; `lines`, each candidate's line as indices into `legal_actions` from the first world's state; `reached` per world, and `offsets` and `rows`, the rows each reached candidate's value is the best of; `states` and `complete`. The GIL is released while it enumerates.
- **Python backend** (`planner.plan(..., backend="rust")`, `PlannerBot(..., backend="rust")`): scores the matrix in one call of the evaluator's `score_rows` (`RowEvaluator`: `value.ValueEvaluator` has it), takes each candidate's best with one `np.maximum.reduceat`, and chooses with the same code as the Python backend. It needs a learned value: the hand-valued `evaluate` scores states, not rows, and stays Python-only. A batch's matrix products can differ from a single row's in the last bit; the scores are rounded to whole points out of a million, so they agree (a test checks a plan's ends).
- **Differential tests** (`tests/star_realms/test_native_planner.py`): on 50 positions (worlds sampled from the turn starts of two seeded heuristic games and a hand-built turn with a draw, 1 to 4 worlds, budgets of 40 to 3,000 so that some run out), the Rust ends must be the states the Python planner scores, in its order, with `features.encode`'s rows bit for bit, the same states visited and the same completeness; whole plans with either backend must be equal (line, value, states, completeness) with both packaged models; and two whole seeded games of the planner (3 worlds, 1,500 states, against greedy and the heuristic) must have identical transcripts, plans and replans. About 6 s.

**Self-play with the value's forward pass (#141), as built.** One call from Python plays a batch of whole seeded games in Rust across threads and returns the training rows and the results. Results: Milestone 7 (self-play) in [star-realms-results.md](star-realms-results.md).

- **Forward pass** (`value.rs`): `Value` holds a model's standardisation and its form, `Form::Logistic { w, b }` or `Form::Mlp { w1, b1, w2, b2 }` (one hidden layer of tanh units, `w1` row-major), and `predict(row)` is `Model.predict` operation for operation: `(x - mean) / std`, the dot product or the hidden layer then the output's, and `sigmoid(z) = 0.5 * (1 + tanh(0.5 * z))`. `score` is `ValueEvaluator`: `WIN` or `-WIN` for a won end, else `SPAN * (2p - 1)` rounded half to even (`round_ties_even`, as Python's `round` and `np.round` round), negated unless the perspective is the player who just ended. Only the order of the products' sums differs from numpy's (blocked and vectorised there, left to right here), so the probabilities agree to about 1e-15, and the whole-point scores the planner compares agree (tests check both, to 1e-12).
- **Choice and bot** (`planner.rs`): `plan(worlds, budget, value)` scores the enumeration's ends row by row and chooses as `planner._choose` does: a candidate is worth, in each world that reached it, the best score among its rows there; the candidates reached in the most worlds compete by their mean, ties to the first found (a strict `>` over candidates in order, as Python's `max` keeps the first); none reached means the greedy move. Scores are whole numbers, so a candidate's sum is exact and its mean the same correctly rounded division as Python's `int / int`: no ordering of floats can differ. `PlannerBot` is `planner.PlannerBot` with the CPython rng port seeded as `random.Random(seed)`: it samples its worlds from its view with it, plays the plan's line while its view, up to pile order (`seen`, the piles sorted by the card enum's order, the same equality as Python's sort by name), is the one the line reached in the first world, and counts its plans, turns and replans.
- **Games and threads** (`selfplay.rs`): a game is `(seed, seat 0, seat 1)` among a batch's players, `Player::Greedy`, `Player::Heuristic` or `Player::Planner { value, worlds, budget }`; seat 0's bot is seeded with the seed and seat 1's with the next, as tournaments seed them. It is played as `fit.boundaries` plays it: each state `EndTurn` leaves (before the next player's automatic play) gives a feature row, and a game past `max_turns` stops unfinished. `play_games` runs std scoped threads (no new dependency), each taking the next unplayed game from an atomic counter, and returns the games in the order asked, so the results are the same with any number of threads. Each thread has its own shuffle cache; the players, and their models, are shared read-only.
- **Boundary**: `predict(model, x)` (the forward pass over a float64 matrix, for the tests) and `self_play(players, games, threads=1, max_turns=300, record=False, judge=False, diagnose=False)`, which releases the GIL for the whole batch. Models cross as dicts of float64 arrays (`selfplay.native_model`: the `.npz` file's arrays, as `value.Model` loads them), so any model, earlier ones included, is just another player. It returns the finished games' rows as one float32 matrix (every feature is a small integer, exact in float32, as `fit.Dataset.x` stores it), each row's label, game, position and the seat that ended it, and per game its winner, turns, and each seat's plans and replans, with every decision if `record`, and with `judge` each row's judgement by the seat that ended it (its value's forward pass on the row, NaN for the heuristic and greedy; #184), and with `diagnose` each row's cards left in each player's deck and each plan's game, turn, seat and completeness (#185). What a game records is one `selfplay::Record` of flags, each off by default, and none changes a game (a test plays the same batch with and without).
- **Python side** (`selfplay.py`): `Planner(model, worlds, budget)`, `"heuristic"` and `"greedy"` are the players; `play(games, threads, max_turns, record)` returns a `SelfPlay` whose `x` and `y` are `fit.Dataset`'s, so `value.fit(form, played.x, played.y, ...)` fits them; `alternating(a, b, seeds)` seats two players as a tournament does. It has no `evaluate` column: the hand-valued `evaluate` is Python-only, and self-play rounds are gated by tournaments, not by the offline gate.
- **Differential tests** (`tests/star_realms/test_selfplay.py`): the forward pass against numpy on random models and inputs and on more than 5,000 real rows (every end the planner lists from a seeded game's turn starts) with both packaged models, the rounded scores equal; heuristic-vs-heuristic games giving exactly `fit.dataset`'s rows, labels and winners; a batch of planner, heuristic and greedy games with identical rows, results, plans and decisions on 1, 2 and 5 threads; and three whole seeded games of the planner (3 worlds, 1,500 states, both models, against greedy and the heuristic, in either seat) with the same decisions, winner, turns, plans and replans as the Python `PlannerBot` with the Rust enumeration and the same model. About 1.5 s.

**Fewer copies (#156), as built.** The planner had been bound by copying states: every pile was a `Vec`, so a copy was 16 allocations, and the planner copied more (merge keys, ends, its caches). Results: Milestone 7 (fewer state copies) in [star-realms-results.md](star-realms-results.md): about 2.9 times faster per state, and a 20,000-game self-play round in about 25 minutes instead of 1.2 hours.

- **Inline piles** (`state.rs`): every pile is a `Pile<N>`, an inline list of at most `N` cards, `Copy`, read as a slice; equality, hashing and `Debug` see only its cards, never the places left over, and pushing past the capacity panics. The capacities are bounded by the card counts (`state::capacity`, which a test checks against the card data): a player owns at most 100 cards (10 starting, the 80-card Trade Deck, 10 Explorers), so a deck, hand or discard pile holds at most 100, ships in play at most 75 and bases at most the Trade Deck's 25; the Trade Deck 80, the Trade Row 5, the scrap heap 100 (Explorers go back to their pile); allies used and pending choices at most one per card in play, 100; bases used at most the bases in play, 25. A state is 2,560 bytes of plain data, and a copy allocates nothing. No new crate: the type is about 150 lines. Views keep `Vec`s, which are built once per decision, not per state.
- **Tables of indices** (`planner.rs`): the planner keeps each state it lists once, in a list (the shared part's states, and per world the states within the turn and the ends), and its tables map a hash to the latest index with it, chaining the others, so no table holds a state. `merge_hash` hashes a state from its fields without copying it: a pile whose order doesn't matter as a sum of its cards' mixed hashes (SplitMix64's finaliser), which no order changes; a deck (and the Trade Deck) by its length and its top four cards in order, since within a world's turn decks change only at the top. `merged` compares two states field by field, the order-free piles as multisets (counting for up to 16 cards, sorted copies beyond), and is exactly the old merge key's equality (a test checks it, and that merged states hash alike, over thousands of states). Both destructure every field by name, so a field added to `GameState` doesn't compile until they handle it.
- **States made in place**: a play's state is pushed onto the list as a copy of its parent's and the play applied there; if it merges with a state listed before it is popped again. So a state is copied once per play tried, and kept only if new. The lists are reserved up front (to the budget, at most 25,000 states), so they rarely grow. Other worlds replay the shared states their candidates start from into a list of their own, found by index.
- **Unchanged**: every rule, the walk, the order of the ends and the plans; every differential test passes unchanged.

## Learned values: training, models and data

- **What is learned**: V(s), the probability that the player who has just ended their turn goes on to win, for states s at turn boundaries only: the state `EndTurn` leaves, before the next player's automatic play (which would reveal their hand). Those are exactly the states the whole-turn planner scores, so V never has to judge the middle of a turn, and within-turn tactics are the planner's job.
- **Features are order-invariant** (`features.encode`, one numpy row per state, from the side of the player who just ended, then the player to move): per player, a count of each card owned by zone (the draw pool, bases in play), Authority, base defense and the discards they owe, plus the turn and a count of each card in the Trade Row: 190 features. The draw pool is deck, hand, discard pile and cards in play: at a turn boundary the hand was just drawn from the deck, so which cards it holds is part of the deck's order. Never the order of a deck. The planner scores *sampled* worlds, and a value that could read deck order would score the sample instead of the position.
- **Hand-made features** (#173, `features.EXTRA`, `features.extra`): 28 more, computed from a row's counts alone, so stored rows need no regenerating. Per player: combat per hand (5 × the pool's primary combat / its size, plus the bases in play's combat), the kill threat (that less the other player's Authority and base defense), and per faction the pool's density, the expected same-faction pairs in a hand of 5 (ally abilities fire on pairs) and the faction's bases in play. A model opts in (`value.Model.extra`, `fit(..., extra=features.EXTRA)`, saved as an `extra` array; a model without it is saved and loads as before) and appends them to each row before standardising, so the planner still hands it the 190 counts. The Rust forward pass computes them from card tables Python hands over (`features.extra_tables`), bit-identically (every sum is of small integers, with one division each), and the differential tests cover them. Measured offline on one round of pi03's games (results, Milestone 8: value capacity and hand-made features): at width 32 they lowered training and held-out log-loss by about 0.015, below pi03's own on its states, and took the candidate from 40.7% to **50.3%** against pi03 (64 seeds won only with them, 35 only without, z ≈ 2.9), at 1.45× the planner's cost. The value needed ratios of counts it couldn't build from standardised counts. Level with pi03 isn't beating it, so nothing was promoted then. **At the window's size they beat pi03** (#175, results, Milestone 8: hand-made features at pi03's data size): fitted to run 2's saved rounds 1-4 (369,419 states, no games played), the 4-member candidate with them won 56.3% (50.7-61.8%) against pi03 on 300 seeds where the same fit without them won 44.7% (71 seeds won only with them, 36 only without, z ≈ 3.4), passed the SPRT gate (H1 after 549 games, 55.2%), won 54.0% (49.6-58.3%) on 500 fresh seeds and 74.0% against the heuristic, and was promoted as `pf01`. **`iterate` takes them** (`--extra`, `Settings.extra`; off by default, so earlier runs resume unchanged): both forms' fits then read them. No run has used it yet. **More of them** (#178, `features.MORE`, `features.extra(x, more=True)`): 17 more from the counts, appended after `EXTRA`'s by a model that opts in (`value.EXTRAS`: none, `EXTRA`, or `EXTRA` then `MORE`; `pf01`'s saved form is unchanged and pinned by a test). Per player trade and Authority per hand, the pool's densities of cards that draw, scrap or make the opponent discard and of bases, and the Outposts' defense; then the Authority, combat-per-hand and trade-per-hand leads. The Rust forward pass computes them bit-identically from tables under `extra_tables(more=True)["more"]`. Offline on #175's data (results, Milestone 8: more hand-made features, and a cheaper pf01) they lowered the losses by about 0.001 and didn't move the match: 49.3% against pf01, against 48.3% for the same fit without them (32 seeds won only with them, 29 only without), so nothing was promoted. The same test found **one member as strong as four**: a one-member fit of pf01's form drew level with pf01 (49.7%, 44.0-55.3%) at about 0.55× its time per plan; not substituted, as replacing the champion with a cheaper, level model is the owner's call.
- **Targets are game results** (Monte Carlo regression): each turn-boundary state is labelled with whether that player won. It is unbiased and has no bootstrapping to destabilise it; temporal-difference targets are the refinement if variance turns out to be the limit. The first data are seeded heuristic-vs-heuristic games (`fit.boundaries`, `--epsilon` for random moves, unused so far). `fit` and the committed models use these targets, and the policy-iteration loop by default.
- **Temporal-difference targets** (#165, `td.targets`): a state's label is its TD(λ) return, `G_t = (1 − λ)(1 − V(s_t+1)) + λ(1 − G_t+1)` back from the game's end, where `s_t+1` is the next turn-boundary state of the same game, V the current value, and the `1 − ...` the change of side, since the opponent left that state. A game's last state takes its result, whatever λ, and λ = 1 gives back the Monte Carlo targets. Why: after two policy-iteration runs the candidates' variance came from the data and the noise of single game results, not the fit's seed, and a bootstrapped value averages over the many games that pass through similar next states. Self-play and `fit.Dataset` record each state's `game` and `position` in it for this. Tested offline on one dataset of pi03's games (results, Milestone 8: temporal-difference targets): every λ from 0 to 0.95 beat Monte Carlo targets on the same data against pi03 (44.7-48.0% against 39.3%, 300 games each; 0.8 best), but none beat pi03, so nothing was promoted. **`iterate` takes them** (#170, `--targets td --lam 0.8`; the default stays `mc`, so earlier runs resume unchanged): each round, every window round's training targets are rebuilt from the current best's values of that round's states (`iterate.targets`, one round's games at a time, from the `game_train` and `position_train` its saved data now holds), so the bootstrap is always the champion the candidate must beat. Why in a run: the offline test fitted one round's data, and pi03 was fitted to three times as much; the 7 points TD gained there could be enough at the window's size. Held-out log-loss, and so the offline gate, stays on game results. **Run 3** (#170, results, Milestone 8: policy-iteration run 3) played exactly run 2's first three rounds' games with TD(0.8) targets: nothing was promoted (the one candidate to reach a match lost, 46.7% after 255 games), and on the same seeds it did no better than run 2's Monte Carlo candidate (119 against 130 wins in 255). The offline gain at one round's data didn't carry over to three rounds' data, so `--targets` stays `mc` by default.
- **Form**: logistic regression on the features first. `evaluate` is already linear, but in a handful of hand-set weights that tournament tuning couldn't improve (#115). One weight per card, fitted to outcomes, is the smallest change that tests whether the weights or the linear form is the limit. A small network comes next, for nonlinear effects such as faction synergy. Both are built (`value.Logistic`, `value.Mlp`: one hidden layer of tanh units, 32 by default), and both beat `evaluate`, the network more (results, Milestone 8: offline value fit). **Capacity isn't the limit** (#173, results, Milestone 8: value capacity and hand-made features): on one round of pi03's games, today's form's training log-loss is well below its held-out one (0.506 against 0.529), so it isn't underfitting; 64 and 128 hidden units didn't move the training loss at the fit's settings and played no better against pi03 (42.3% and 39.0% against 40.7%, 300 paired games) at 1.7× and 2.7× the planner's cost. So the width stays 32, and two hidden layers weren't built (that step was conditional on width helping or on underfitting).
- **Ensembles** (#164): a model may hold several members, fits of one form to the same rows from different seeds (`value.fit_ensemble`; they share the standardisation, which depends only on the rows), and its value is the mean of their win probabilities. Fits from scratch on similar data played very differently in the first policy-iteration run (35.6% to 60.5% against the champion), so a candidate that is one fit is partly a draw of the seed; averaging k of them cuts the part of that variance the seed causes, at k times the forward pass's cost. Measured (results, Milestone 8: ensemble candidates and policy-iteration run 2), the seed's part turned out to be small, about 3 points of win rate. Four members removed it and added about 5 points of strength, at 1.75× the planner's cost per move, but the candidates still varied from round to round as much as before: that variance comes from the data, not the seed. `--members` defaults to 1. A one-member model is a single fit, saved exactly as before (`param_<name>` arrays); one of several saves `member_<i>_<name>` arrays and their count, `members`. Both forward passes take the members (the Rust one sums their probabilities in member order and divides by their count, as numpy does), and the differential tests cover an ensemble too.
- **The offline gate**: a candidate value is kept only if its held-out log-loss beats `sigmoid(evaluate / 16)` on the same states. Fitting and scoring take minutes, so forms and features are compared without tournaments. The `fit` command (`python -m minimax_games.games.star_realms fit`, `task fit`) does it all from seeds: it plays the training games and the held-out games after them, fits both forms, and prints each one's held-out log-loss beside `evaluate`'s and a constant's. At the defaults (8,000 training games, 2,000 held out) it takes under a minute with `--jobs 2`.
- **Training is hand-written in numpy**: a logistic regression's gradient is closed-form, and a one-hidden-layer network's backpropagation and optimiser are a few dozen lines, checked against finite-difference gradients. `value.fit` standardises the features, then runs Adam on shuffled minibatches against the mean log-loss plus an L2 penalty on the weights, deterministic given its seed. At tens of thousands of parameters and up to a million states it trains on a laptop CPU in minutes. No ML framework is a dependency. One becomes a dependency, in its own uv dependency group and never in the core, only if a network is too large or too varied to write by hand.
- **Inference** is the same arithmetic in two places: numpy in the Python planner, for trying a new form of value, and a hand-written forward pass in the Rust engine, for self-play and play at speed. Both read the same model file, and a test checks they agree. Both are built (the Rust one in #141, Compiled engine: self-play): `value.ValueEvaluator(model)` is an `evaluate.Evaluator`, the model's probability mapped to `-SPAN..SPAN` as rollouts map theirs, negated for the player to move, and its `score_rows` scores a whole feature matrix the same way, for the planner's Rust enumeration (#140).
- **Models are `.npz` files** of plain arrays (the form's name, the feature names, the standardisation and the parameters), readable by numpy and by the Rust forward pass, never pickles (which run code when loaded). A model that beats `evaluate`, or becomes a rung of the ladder, is a result: it is committed in `src/minimax_games/games/star_realms/models/`, with the command, commit and seeds that trained it recorded in [star-realms-results.md](star-realms-results.md), and loaded by name (`value.load("mlp")`). **The ladder's current best is `pf01`** (#175, results, Milestone 8: hand-made features at pi03's data size): a 4-member ensemble at width 32 with the hand-made features, fitted offline to run 2's rounds 1-4 of pi03's games. Its planner (`planner:pf01`) beat pi03's in the SPRT gate (H1 after 549 games, 55.2%) and 54.0% (49.6-58.3%) on 500 fresh seeds, and the heuristic 74.0% (70.0-77.7%) on seeds 0-499, against pi03's 70.2% there; it costs about 2× pi03's time per move. Below it, `pi03`, promoted in round 3 of the first policy-iteration run: the planner with it (`planner:pi03`) beat the planner with `mlp` 60.5% in its gating match and 58.0% (53.6-62.2%) on 500 fresh seeds, and the heuristic 70.2% on seeds 0-499, level with `mlp`'s 69.0% there (results, Milestone 8: policy iteration). The second run, which started from `pi03` with candidates that were ensembles of 4 fits, promoted nothing: its seven candidates went 39.7-53.1% against it (results, Milestone 8: ensemble candidates and policy-iteration run 2). The third, with TD(0.8) targets, promoted nothing either: its one candidate to reach a match lost 46.7% (results, Milestone 8: policy-iteration run 3). The rungs below `pi03`: `mlp` (beat the planner on `evaluate` 62.8%; Milestone 8: the planner with a learned value), then `evaluate`. A test loads each committed model and checks it was fitted to the current features, so changing the encoder means refitting them. The `check-added-large-files` hook is the size limit; a model past it is attached to a GitHub Release instead.
- **Training data is never committed.** Games are deterministic given their seeds and bots, so a dataset is fully described by the command that generates it.

### Policy iteration (#142)

Each round plays self-play games with the current best value, refits on recent games, and promotes the refit only if it beats the current best. A round is sized to take about 10-12 minutes on the laptop (self-play about 14 games/s on 8 threads, #156), so the loop stays tight.

- **Round size: 5,000 games.** About 6 minutes of play and a minute of fitting. With the window below, each fit still sees about 20,000 games (about 500,000 states), more than the 8,000 that gave `mlp`, and the network gained most from more data.
- **Opponents:** half the games are the current best against itself, a quarter against the heuristic, a quarter against an earlier champion chosen uniformly, seats alternating. Self-play explores the positions the value leads to. The heuristic keeps it honest against a fixed, different style: a learned agent can lose to a scripted bot left out of its training (Gerigk & Engels, Dominion). Earlier champions guard against forgetting how to beat older styles and against strategies cycling. No random moves at first: sampled worlds and the mix already vary the positions; they are added only if fits start to overfit.
- **Data: a sliding window of the last 4 rounds, refitted from scratch.** A fresh fit is deterministic and takes seconds, with no drift from warm starts, and older rounds, played by weaker values, age out. Both forms are fitted each round; the better held-out log-loss goes forward.
- **Gating, in two stages.** First offline: on held-out games from the newest round, the candidate's log-loss must beat the current best's on the same states, which costs nothing and skips a match it would lose. Then a match: the candidate's planner against the current best's, same settings, both on the Rust backend, stopped by SPRT with H0 50% against H1 55%, α = β = 0.05, at most 1,000 games; undecided at the limit is a failure. A promoted value also plays a fixed 500 games against the heuristic on seeds 0-499, recorded on the ladder but not gating. A candidate that fails is dropped and doesn't join the opponent pool, which holds champions only; its round's games stay in the window.
- **Seeds** for training, held-out, gating and ladder games come from disjoint ranges, so no value is judged on games it was trained on.
- **Ideas are tested offline before they get a run.** A change to the value's form, targets or features is first fitted on a fixed dataset of the champion's games and played against the champion in a short fixed-seed match of a few hundred games in Rust, which takes minutes. Log-loss alone won't do: in both runs the offline gate passed candidates that then lost. A policy-iteration run is spent only on a change that has already beaten the champion that way. Both runs show why: a run of rounds is the slow way to learn that an idea doesn't help, since each round costs about 10 minutes and a plateau takes 3 of them to show.
- **Stopping:** after 3 rounds, or 3 rounds in a row without a promotion, or once the rounds have taken 30 minutes, whichever comes first. That keeps a run inside the repo's feedback-loop limit. A run that is still promoting at the end resumes with the same `--out` for 3 more rounds (`--rounds 6`). That is a new decision, taken on the evidence of the rounds so far, and a run never resumes past the patience rule, because a plateau points at the form, not the data: a wider network, temporal-difference targets or richer features.
- **Records:** every round's games, log-losses, gate result and time go in the results doc; each promoted model is committed as a ladder rung; and the per-seed results of gating and ladder matches are committed, so later bots can be paired against them.

**As built** (`iterate.py`, `python -m minimax_games.games.star_realms iterate`, `task iterate`):

- **A round** is one `selfplay.play` call for its 5,000 games, **4,000 training and 1,000 held-out**, each set mixed by `iterate.mix`: the first half the current best against itself, the next quarter against the heuristic, the rest against a champion drawn uniformly per game from the pool (an rng seeded with the set's first seed), the current best moving first in alternate games against each opponent. The pool holds every champion, the current best included, so round 1's quarter is `mlp` against itself too. The held-out games are never trained on, so a fit sees the window's 16,000 training games (about 370,000 states), not 20,000.
- **The fit** (`iterate.fit_candidates`): both forms from scratch with `value.fit`'s defaults (seed 0, 10 epochs, batch 256, Adam at 0.001, `l2` 0.003, 32 hidden units), on the training rows of the last 4 rounds, each an ensemble of `--members` k fits from seeds 0 to k − 1 (`Settings.members`, default 1: a single fit, as in the first run; a run recorded before the setting existed resumes as one member), fitted to game results or, with `--targets td`, to TD(`--lam`) returns bootstrapping from the current best (a run recorded before the setting existed resumes with game results); reading the hand-made features too with `--extra` (`Settings.extra`, default off; a run recorded before the setting existed resumes without them); the form with the lower log-loss on this round's held-out rows is the candidate, named `<prefix><round>` (`pi01`, `pi02`, ...).
- **The match** is played in Rust, not through `tournament.play_sprt`: in batches of 128 games (`selfplay.play`, the candidate first in alternate games), with the SPRT (`sprt.Sprt`) applied game by game in seed order and the games after the deciding one dropped, so the decision and games are the ones a game-by-game match would give. Through `play_sprt` (8 processes, the Python planner with the Rust enumeration) planner-against-planner games ran at 9.1 games/s against 11.5 in one Rust batch of 64, and Rust needs one process, not eight.
- **Seeds**: the ladder 0-499; round `r`'s training games from 1,000,000 + 10,000 (r − 1), its held-out games from 2,000,000 + 10,000 (r − 1), its match from 3,000,000 + 10,000 (r − 1) (`iterate.Settings`: `train_seeds`, `held_out_seeds`, `gate_seeds`). Every range is clear of the other ranges and of earlier tournaments and fits (all below 60,000); a test checks they are disjoint for up to 100 rounds.
- **Stopping** (`iterate.stop_reason`), checked before each round: `--rounds` played (default 3), `--patience` rounds in a row without a promotion (default 3), or the rounds so far having taken `--hours` (default 0.5, so the last round may run past it). The first two runs used 10 rounds and 2 hours.
- **Durable and resumable.** Everything goes in the run's directory (`--out`, `runs/iterate/` by default, which git ignores), written as it completes: `state.json` (the settings and each finished round's record: games, states, the window, every log-loss, the gate's results, the ladder's, and the seconds each stage took), `data/round-NN.npz` (the round's rows, about 85 MB, so an interrupted round doesn't replay its games and later rounds' windows load them), `candidates/` (both forms' fits), `models/<name>.npz` (each promoted model, which later rounds load by name before the packaged ones), and the per-seed CSVs `gate-rNN.csv` and `ladder-rNN.csv` (seed, first, second, winner, turns). Running again with the same `--out` resumes after the last finished round; the settings that change a game, fit or decision must match, and only the threads and stopping rules may change. A run's `state.json`, log and CSVs are then committed in `docs/policy-iteration/run-N/`, and its promoted models in `models/`.
- **Tests** (`tests/star_realms/test_iterate.py`, about 1 s): the mix's shares, seats and uniform draws; disjoint seeds; the window; the gates' decisions (strictly lower log-loss; the SPRT stopping at the deciding game, unfinished games counting toward the limit, undecided at the limit failing); the stopping rules; and whole runs of tiny rounds (a few games, 60-state plans): records and CSVs, a promotion's wiring (the champion and pool change, the ladder plays seeds 0 up), a failed candidate dropped, resuming after finished rounds without replaying them, reusing an interrupted round's games, refusing to resume with other settings (target settings included); and the targets: Monte Carlo fits see the window's game results unchanged, TD fits see `td.targets` of each window round's rows with the current champion's values.

### The exploitation gap (#184)

The diagnostic for What it learns from's second source (`gap.py`, `python -m minimax_games.games.star_realms gap`). Results: Milestone 9 (the exploitation gap) in [star-realms-results.md](star-realms-results.md).

- **Games**: the champion's own, mixed as a policy-iteration round mixes them (`iterate.mix`: half against itself, a quarter against the heuristic, a quarter against a champion drawn from the pool, every champion by default), at the planner's full settings, on seeds from 18,400,000, clear of every range a champion was fitted to. Self-play plays them with `judge` (and `diagnose`, for the other diagnostics), so nothing but the recording is new and no game changes.
- **The judgement of an end** is the player's value on the position its turn actually ended in, the same row it is trained on. Not the plan's value, the mean over sampled worlds of each world's best end: that judges a line, not a position, it is replaced whenever the player replans within a turn, and the opponents' ends have no plan to compare it with. The value of the position played is selected just as the plan's value is (the planner chose the turn whose ends it judged best), so it carries the selection effect the gap measures.
- **Held out**: the same champion's judgement of the ends its opponents chose (the heuristic and the earlier champions; never the champion's own seat, so mirror games give none). Those positions weren't selected by its judgement, so their calibration is plain miscalibration; the gap on chosen positions beyond it, bin for bin of judged chance, is the selection effect. Both sets are reported side by side.
- **Reported**: the gap (mean judged chance less the share of positions whose player went on to win) overall, by bins of judged chance (tenths: the calibration table) and by game phase (the game's turn, both players' turns counted: 1-6, 7-12, 13-18, 19-24, 25 on), each with its count. Its standard error counts a game's positions together (cluster-robust by game), because they share one result; a bin from a single game shows an error of 0.

### The other diagnostics (#185)

The judgement's error near a reshuffle and on rare cards, and the turns not examined in full (How progress is measured; `diagnostics.py`, `python -m minimax_games.games.star_realms diagnose`). Results: Milestone 9 (the judgement's errors by reshuffle distance and rare cards, and turns not examined) in [star-realms-results.md](star-realms-results.md).

- **Games**: the exploitation gap's, played the same way (`gap.measure`), with self-play's `diagnose` recording what the breakdowns need and nothing else, so no game changes: at each turn's end, each player's cards left to draw before their discard pile is reshuffled, and each plan's turn and whether it examined every end of the turn in every world. By default the command plays every third game of the gap's 1,000 (`--every 3`): the same games, in the mix's proportions, in a third of the time, which keeps it in minutes.
- **Error** is reported two ways in every bin: the gap (mean judged chance less share won), which shows a judgement that is consistently too high or too low, and the mean squared miss (the Brier score), which shows a judgement that is merely noisier there and whose misses cancel in the gap. Both carry the cluster-robust error by game.
- **Cards left before a reshuffle** are the cards in a player's deck, not counting the hand: at a turn's end the player who ended has just drawn their next hand, and the next player holds the hand they drew at their own last end, so in both cases the deck is what their next draws take before the discard pile is shuffled. Fewer than 5 is **near**: the player's next hand draw reshuffles, so whatever they buy on their coming turn is in the new deck at once; from 5 up it waits at least a hand longer (**far**). It is reported for the player who just ended and for the next player separately, in bins 0-4, 5-9, 10-14 and 15 on, and far (5 on) as one row. The judgement sees each player's draw pool as one set (What it sees), so if when a card will be drawn matters, its error should be larger near a reshuffle than far from one.
- **A rare card** is a Trade Deck card with one copy (23 of the base set's 46 distinct Trade Deck cards). A position has one when either player holds one, in their draw pool or in play as a base; one in the Trade Row isn't held. A card with one copy is seen in few training positions, so if cards are learned only by name, the error should be larger where one is held.
- **Within each game phase.** The game's phase confounds both comparisons: decks grow and rare cards are bought as a game goes on, while early positions are the hardest to call (their squared miss is near a coin flip's 0.25 whatever the judgement). So near against far and a rare card against none are also given phase by phase on the chosen positions, and only a difference that holds within phases speaks for the belief.
- **A turn not examined in full** is one of the champion's turns in which some plan ran out of budget before listing every end of the turn in some world (`planner.Plan.complete`). A turn has one plan per chance event to plan past, and one more each time the game goes otherwise than planned; it counts as examined in full only if all of them were. Reported as the share of the champion's turns by game phase, with the counts.

### Looking through the reply (#188)

How it chooses' first test, its second half: does choosing by the judgement after the opponent's reply, rather than after the player's own turn, change the champion's choice and add strength at equal cost? Results: Milestone 9 (looking through the reply) in [star-realms-results.md](star-realms-results.md).

- **Only the top candidates are looked through.** The plan is made as today (each candidate, a line up to the next chance event, is worth the mean over worlds of its best end by the judgement); then the best **3** candidates are each worth the mean over worlds of the player's chance after the opponent replies from that candidate's best end in that world, and the best of those is played. The belief is that the judgement already summarises the reply, so the only choices the reply could fairly overturn are among candidates the judgement rates nearly alike; a reply that rescued a candidate far behind would itself be the belief failing, which the rank of the reply's pick (below) shows. One reply per candidate per world, from its best end: within a candidate the ends differ only after the player's chance event, and the best end there is the one the player would play once it has seen the draw.
- **The opponent replies as the planner plans a turn.** From the end of the player's turn in a sampled world, the opponent's hand is that world's (they drew it as the player's turn ended, and they know it), and its deck order is sampled again for 2 sub-worlds; it plans its turn from those as the planner does (its line up to its first chance event chosen by the mean over sub-worlds, its turn ending where the planner's would), choosing by the same judgement from its side, and the player's chance after the reply is 1 less that judgement of where its turn ends. Solving the reply within the player's own world instead would let the opponent see what its own draws bring, the clairvoyance (a form of strategy fusion) that sampling worlds is known for ([ismcts.md](ismcts.md)); planning it as the planner plans keeps the reply's strategy fusion to what the player's own turn already carries, none before the first chance event, so the two turns are judged by one rule and the comparison isn't tilted towards either.
- **Equal cost, in states.** Each reply is given 2,500 states per sub-world, 5,000 in all, so a plan costs at most its own 20,000 states and 12 replies (3 candidates × 4 worlds), **80,000**: four times today's. It is compared with the plain planner given the same 80,000 as **16 worlds** at 5,000 states each, the plain planner's own way to spend the same budget (results, Milestone 9: more sampled worlds; the SPRT of Milestone 10 finds it worth no measurable strength over 4 worlds). States are the repo's deterministic budget; both report the states they used and their time per plan, since replies on small turns finish early. Four times keeps the match in minutes; replies at the planner's full budget would make it thirteen times. The cost is that a large opponent turn isn't examined in full, and a reply cut short plays the best end found so far, underrating the opponent there; the share of replies examined in full, by game phase, says where that happens.
- **Built into the Rust planner**, an option of the planner player in self-play (replies on or off, the candidates, the sub-worlds and the reply's budget), not orchestrated from Python: the reply is the same enumeration the planner already runs, and the match is played in one Rust batch on several threads. Self-play records, per plan of the reply player, whether the reply's pick differs from the judgement's, the pick's rank among the 3, and whether every reply was examined in full, so one match gives both the strength and the changed choices.
- **The match**: 150 seeds, each played in both seats (300 games), paired seed by seed, the reply player against the 16-world planner, both `pf01`. 300 games rule out a gap of about 6 points, which is the size of a "large" gain; a borderline result is for an SPRT to follow.
- **Reading it.** The belief **holds** if the reply player doesn't beat 16 worlds (sign z below 2) and its choice changes on no more plans than a fresh 4-world sample changes the plain planner's (11.3%; results, more sampled worlds), so that what changes is noise between near-ties. It **fails** if the reply player wins with sign z of 2 or more: then lookahead pays more than the same budget spent on worlds, and strength moves towards it (adopting it is a decision on the result, in its own PR). Many changed choices with no strength is the diagnostic moving without strength: the reply sees something real that doesn't limit play. A pick that is often the 3rd candidate says 3 was too few, and the share of changed choices is then a floor.

**As built** (`planner.rs`: `Replies`, `reply`, `plan_with`; `selfplay.Reply`; `reply.py` and the `reply` command, `task reply`):

- **The reply's sub-worlds are sampled from the opponent's view of the end, after its automatic play**, as the planner samples its own worlds (`sample_world` of `observe` from the opponent's seat): its hand is the world's and everything it can't see is drawn again, its deck order, the player's new hand and the Trade Deck's order. Resampling only its deck would leave the Trade Deck's order fixed across sub-worlds, so the reply would see the cards a Trade Row refill brings, the same clairvoyance through another pile. The automatic play comes first because the game plays it before the opponent's first decision, and in the world: the opponent sees its result as it does in a game.
- **A plan with fewer than 2 candidates in contention looks through no reply**: with one, no reply can change the choice, so the budget isn't spent. With `candidates` 1 the option is the plain planner, plan for plan; with replies off it is today's planner exactly, and the Rust-against-Python differential tests still pass.
- **Ends that need no reply**: an end that wins, or an automatic play that does, is worth its own judgement (a win's score); a reply that finds no end within its budget (none at the design's settings) falls back to the end's judgement. The plan's states count its replies', so `states per plan` and the 80,000 cap are checked in the tests.
- **Self-play records** every planner's plans (`self_play(..., replies=True)`, `selfplay.Replies`): the states, the candidates looked through, the pick's rank and whether every reply was examined in full, with each seat's time spent planning. A plan's rank above 1 is a changed choice.

## Harness

- A plain match loop (deal; ask the current bot for an action given its view; apply; repeat until someone's Authority reaches 0) and a plain tournament loop over seeds and pairings. No py-ev: the base set has no timing to model.
- **Game log**: a replayable text transcript per seed. It is **opt-in** (off by default, so tournaments don't pay for I/O) and records only the actions the match runner applies, automatic ones marked "(auto)", never search nodes. Nothing is formatted when it's off. `play_match(..., log=sink)` takes a `Callable[[str], None]` sink (e.g. `print` or `list.append`); `transcript.describe(action, state)` formats one action with an exhaustive `match` over `Action`, so each new action kind needs one new case. `python -m minimax_games.games.star_realms --seed 7 --log` prints a game's transcript. Search code logs only behind `logger.isEnabledFor(logging.DEBUG)`.
- **Tournament**: `python -m minimax_games.games.star_realms tournament --bots greedy random --games 500 [--base-seed 0] [--max-turns 300]` plays game `i` from seed `base_seed + i`, bot A moving first in even games and bot B in odd ones. Each bot is built per game from the game seed (seat 0 gets `seed`, seat 1 `seed + 1`, as in the single-game command, so any game replays on its own). It reports win rates overall and by seat, the mean number of turns and each bot's mean milliseconds per decision (time inside `choose`, measured with `time.perf_counter`). The log is off. `--jobs N` plays the games in N processes, each a run of consecutive seeds starting with bot A first, and combines them (`tournament.combine`): the results are the same as one process's, and each bot's time per decision is measured in the process that made it. A game past `--max-turns` is **unfinished**: listed by seed and left out of every win rate, whose denominator (the finished games) is printed. Results (`tournament.TournamentResult`) are identical for the same seeds and bots; timings (`tournament.Timing`) are kept apart because they vary.
- **Stopping early (SPRT)**: `tournament --sprt [--h0 0.5] [--h1 0.55] [--alpha 0.05] [--beta 0.05]` plays the same paired match (`tournament.play_sprt`), with `--games` as its limit, and after each game runs Wald's sequential probability ratio test (`sprt.Sprt`) on bot A's wins and losses (unfinished games count as neither): it stops when the log-likelihood ratio of H1 (A wins `h1` of finished games) over H0 (A wins `h0`) leaves the bounds `log(beta / (1 - alpha))` and `log((1 - beta) / alpha)`. It reports the decision (H0 or H1 accepted, or undecided at the limit), the games played, A's win rate with its 95% Wilson interval, and the ratio and its bounds, then the usual table for the games played. With `--jobs N`, games are played ahead one per task in N processes, but the decision is taken on completed games in seed order and later games are dropped, so the decision, games played and results are the same as one process's. A clear result settles in tens of games (greedy against the heuristic: H0 after 44). Accepting H0 says only that A is nearer `h0` than `h1` (or below both), not that the bots are level.
- A human-playable CLI is deferred. When it comes, it's another bot whose decisions come from `input()`.

6. **Whole-turn planner** (in Python, alongside the first slices of the Rust port, #127), the move-selection layer for learned play: a value function only scores states, and something has to turn it into moves. The planner does that at turn granularity, so a learned value need only be accurate at turn boundaries and within-turn tactics (lethal, combat splits, play and scrap order) are resolved by the planner, not learned. It is built and measured first on the hand-valued `evaluate`. That separates the two explanations for milestone 5's legitimate bots sitting near 34% against the heuristic, the micro-action tree wasting its budget on orderings or the leaf being too weak, and makes planner-plus-`evaluate` the bar a learned value has to beat. One tournament, against the heuristic, on the seeds of ISMCTS's recorded run against it, so the two compare through their common opponent without a costly pairing of two search bots, and stopped as soon as its result is clear (a sequential probability ratio test). The Python planner then serves as the reference the Rust enumeration is differential-tested against. The cheat, sampler and ISMCTS are not ported to Rust: they are frozen baselines whose recorded results stand, re-run only when a question needs them. Done (#134): the planner won 48.3% (43.7-53.0%) of 445 games against the heuristic, against ISMCTS's 34.6%, so the tree wasted the budget more than the leaf limited it (results, Milestone 6: planner vs heuristic).
7. **Rust engine** (#127), sliced as in Compiled engine, in parallel with 6.

Fitting takes minutes, so the choice of form and features costs no tournaments. Done (#135): both beat it, 0.562 and 0.550 against 0.607 (results, Milestone 8: offline value fit);

Done (#136): with the mlp it won 62.8% of 94 games (SPRT, H1 60% over 50%), and 69.0% of 500 against the heuristic, the first legitimate bot above it; the mlp is the ladder's current best (results, Milestone 8: the planner with a learned value);

Built (#142, Learned values: Policy iteration); the first run promoted one value in 6 rounds, `pi03`, 58% against `mlp` but level with it against the heuristic, and stopped on 3 rounds without a promotion (results, Milestone 8: policy iteration). The second, from `pi03` with 4-member ensemble candidates (#164), promoted nothing: the agreed rule stopped it after round 3 (undecided at 1,000 games, 53.1%), and four more rounds from a resume past that rule all failed too (results, Milestone 8: ensemble candidates and policy-iteration run 2). The third, with TD targets, promoted nothing either; `pf01`, with the hand-made features fitted offline to run 2's first four rounds, then beat `pi03` and is the current best (#175). Done: `pf01` is the champion today's player ends on. Whether its cheaper one-fit form should replace it is a refinement of today's player, so it isn't measured (End state, How progress is measured).
9. **The yardstick, and today's champion on it** (End state, How progress is measured): the reference population with its recorded per-seed results, the probes, the diagnostics, and How it chooses' tests (more examination where it runs out, the opponent's reply, more sampled worlds). No player is trained. The end state's conditional parts each wait on one of these measurements (the exploitation gap for learning from rejected positions, the examination test for a preference over moves, the error near a reshuffle for the deck's cycle), so measuring comes before building. Done (#183-#192; results, Milestone 9: the baseline): every end-state test that needs no new player has a number for `pf01`, and none of the three conditional parts is needed: the exploitation gap is +0.002 ± 0.005, four times the examination changes 2.6% of choices and wins nothing, and the error near a reshuffle is no larger than far from one. The probes find two ideas its judgement hasn't built, same-function synergy for cards that draw or scrap and the Trade Row as it bears on the next buyer.
10. **The end state, one belief at a time**, in the order Milestone 9's baseline says matters most. Each step is an experiment by How progress is measured's rule: it names its belief and predicts its effect on strength (against `pf01`'s population results, paired) and on the probe or diagnostic below; a player that gains from an idea described to it directly joins the reference population as an opponent (Time goes to moving towards the end state). The order puts first the parts of the end state that today's player lacks and that a measurement says its judgement is missing, then the parts no measurement yet speaks for:
    1. **The Trade Row as it bears on whoever buys next** (What it sees). First because it is the one part of What it sees that today's description lacks entirely and that a measurement says is missing: the judgement sees about 2% of what a row card is worth to the next buyer, and its buys don't deny. Without it denial can't be represented at all, so the judgement can only learn an average. Its diagnostic is ready: the denial probe's share seen and the share of buys that deny should rise.
    2. **Cards by what they do** (What it sees). Second because the same-function synergy probe finds the judgement hasn't built the worth of a second card that draws or scraps, which knowing only cards' names can't give it; the rare-card diagnostic doesn't call for it, and #178's densities of drawing and scrapping cards added no strength, so its case rests on one probe and comes after the row's. Its diagnostic: the synergy probe's second card against the first, against the control.
    3. **More sampled worlds, settled** (How it chooses). An SPRT of 16 worlds against 4 (H0 50%, H1 55%), the only How it chooses test left undecided (54.3%, sign z 1.90). Third, not first, though it takes minutes: it refines how today's judgement is used rather than what the player knows, at most a few points at four times the time per plan, and a slower player is a cost to every later experiment (the end state's constraint), so it would change matches only, not learning. Done (#210; results, Milestone 10: 16 worlds against 4, settled): H0 accepted after 200 games (16 worlds won 43.5%; with #186's games, exactly 50%), so 4 worlds stays.
    4. **How it explores and the judgement's confidence** (How it explores; What it judges, the judgement knows how sure it is). Last because they need players trained alike with and without them, the costliest experiments, and no measurement yet points to them: `pf01` judges investment as strong players do, so the probe shows no sign that a lack of exploration limited it, and its exploitation gap is near zero, so caution in matches has little overestimation to remove.

    Not steps: learning from rejected positions, a preference over moves and the deck's cycle (not needed on Milestone 9's measurements; each is re-measured on every new champion and becomes a step if its measurement moves), and proportions, which today's player already has (the hand-made features).

## Why not...

- **Chance nodes / expectiminimax**: they would change `Node` and `alphabeta`. Pruning with chance nodes is weak, and almost every draw in Star Realms is a chance event. Sampling worlds keeps `search/` two-player.
- **Rejecting whole-turn moves because of mid-turn draws**: within a turn the only hidden information is the order of the player's own deck and of the Trade Deck, and each card is seen the moment it is drawn or revealed. So the planner commits only up to the next chance event and samples beyond it (Search, whole-turn planner). Listing a turn's outcomes is a search over micro-actions, but a cheap one: the transposition table merges the orderings that reach the same end of turn.
- **Stopping the plan at the first chance event**: the prefix would have to be scored mid-turn, and the planner exists so that the value function need only be accurate at turn boundaries.
- **Expectimax over turn segments** (branching on each draw and reveal, then planning the rest under each): exact, but its cost multiplies with every chance event in the turn. Sampling whole worlds is exact with one chance event left and biased upward only by later ones. Expectimax is the refinement to measure against it if that bias shows, for example on draw-heavy turns.
- **Mutable state with apply/undo**: faster, but undo across hidden information, reshuffles and RNG state is bug-prone, and one silent bug invalidates every win-rate comparison. The objection is weaker for the Rust engine than for the Python one: the Python engine stays the oracle, and the differential tests, which compare every state, end and plan bit for bit, would catch a silent undo bug. #156 cut copying with inline piles and tables of indices instead (Compiled engine, "Fewer copies"); apply/undo in the planner's search stays the next lever if self-play must be faster still.
